# Inside the Wire: Resistance and Resistivity

Narration script, draft 1. English, about 10 minutes.

Lines in plain text are spoken. Lines marked **On screen** describe the picture and are not spoken.

---

## 1. The paradox

**On screen:** A hand flips a switch; a lamp across the room lights at once. The camera dives into the wire, through the insulation, down to the atomic scale. Caption: *1 A in a 1 mm² copper wire*.

Flip a switch, and the lamp is on before your finger has left the button. So the electrons must race through the wire. Right?

Let's go inside and look.

Here they are. And they are barely moving. In an ordinary copper wire carrying one ampere, the electrons drift forward at less than a tenth of a millimetre every second. A snail is more than ten times faster. To travel a single metre of this wire, an electron needs nearly four hours.

So how does the lamp light instantly? And what is holding the electrons back so badly?

The answer to both is one idea: resistance. By the end of this video you will be able to see it, not just calculate it.

---

## 2. Inside the metal

**On screen:** A glowing grid of copper ions stretching into the distance. Electrons appear as bright points streaking in every direction. A counter totals leftward and rightward movers: they cancel. Then a battery connects, a soft field washes along the wire, and the whole swarm leans one way. Finally, a pipe already full of water: push in at one end, water leaves the other at once.

This is copper, magnified a hundred million times.

The copper atoms sit in a neat, repeating grid called a lattice. Each atom has let go of one outer electron. Those freed electrons belong to no atom in particular. They wander through the whole metal like a gas, and there is an enormous number of them: about eighty-five billion billion in a speck of copper one millimetre wide.

And they are not resting. Even with no battery connected, every electron is flying around at more than a thousand kilometres per second. But the directions are random. For every electron heading left, another is heading right. Add them all up and the net flow is zero. No current.

Now connect a battery. It sets up an electric field along the wire, and every electron feels a gentle push in one direction. The frantic random motion carries on as before, but now the whole crowd leans, ever so slightly, one way. That slow lean is called drift. And drift is the current.

This also solves our paradox. The wire is already full of electrons, from end to end. Push at one end, and the push is passed along at close to the speed of light. Think of a pipe that is already full of water. Open the tap, and water comes out of the far end at once, even though the water that just entered is still far behind.

The electrons crawl. The message flies.

---

## 3. What resistance is

**On screen:** One electron is followed in close-up. It speeds up along the field, scatters off a trembling ion, flies off sideways, speeds up again. Its zig-zag path is drawn behind it. Then the lattice freezes perfectly still and the electron glides straight through. Finally V, I and R appear as a push, a flow and a ratio.

If the field keeps pushing, why don't the electrons keep getting faster?

Because they keep being interrupted.

The lattice is never perfectly still. Its atoms tremble with heat, and here and there sits an impurity or a flaw. An electron picks up a little speed from the field, then scatters off one of these disturbances and flies off in a random direction. Its forward progress is wiped out, and it has to start again.

Gain, scatter, reset. Gain, scatter, reset. Tens of trillions of times every second.

Here is something most textbooks leave out. The electrons are not simply bumping into the atoms. A perfectly regular, perfectly still lattice would let them glide straight through. What scatters them is the disorder: the trembling, and the flaws. Keep that in mind. It matters later.

This constant interruption is resistance. It is how hard a conductor makes it to keep charge flowing.

We measure it by asking a simple question: how much push do I need for each unit of flow? The push is voltage, in volts. The flow is current, in amperes. Divide one by the other and you get resistance, in ohms.

If one volt drives one ampere, that is one ohm. If you need ten volts for that same ampere, that is ten ohms.

This is Ohm's law: V equals I times R.

## 4. Shape matters

**On screen:** The wire stretches to twice its length while a resistance meter doubles. It snaps back, then swells to twice its cross-section; the electrons spread into more lanes and the meter halves. The formula fragment *R ∝ L / A* is written out. Then a strain gauge on a bridge beam: the beam flexes, the fine wire stretches, the reading ticks up.

Now let's change the wire and watch what happens.

First, make it twice as long. Every electron must now get through twice as much lattice, and is interrupted twice as many times along the way. For the same current, you need twice the push. Double the length, double the resistance.

Next, go back, and make the wire thicker instead, with twice the cross-section. Now there are twice as many electrons side by side, all drifting together, like a road that has gone from two lanes to four. The same push moves twice the charge. Double the area, half the resistance.

Notice that it is the area that counts, not the diameter. Double the diameter and the area becomes four times bigger, so the resistance falls to a quarter.

So resistance grows with length, L, and shrinks with area, A.

Engineers use this every day. A thin extension cord warms up under a heavy load, while a thick one stays cool. And a strain gauge, the sensor that weighs trucks and watches over bridges, is just a very fine wire. Stretch it, and it becomes longer and thinner, so its resistance rises by an amount you can measure.

## 5. Material matters

**On screen:** Two identical wires side by side, copper and nichrome, with very different meter readings. The symbol ρ arrives and completes the formula *R = ρ L / A*; ρ glows in one colour, L/A in another. A one-metre cube of material with a meter across two faces. Then the resistivity ladder: silver, copper, gold, aluminium, nichrome, silicon, glass, on a scale that has to keep zooming out.

But length and area are not the whole story.

Take two wires of exactly the same size, one made of copper and one of nichrome, the alloy inside a heater coil. The nichrome has about sixty-five times the resistance. Same shape. Different material.

So we need a number that describes the material alone, with the shape taken out. That number is resistivity, written with the Greek letter rho.

And now the formula is complete: R equals rho, times L over A.

Read it like this. Rho is what the material contributes. L over A is what the shape contributes.

What is rho, physically? Picture a cube of the material, one metre on each side. The resistance from one face to the opposite face is its resistivity. That is why its unit is the ohm metre.

Here is the distinction to remember. Resistance belongs to an object: this wire, this coil. Resistivity belongs to a substance: copper, anywhere, in any shape. You will also meet its inverse, conductivity, written sigma.

Now look at the range. Silver is the best conductor, with copper just behind, then gold and aluminium. Nichrome is about sixty-five times copper. Pure silicon is more than a hundred billion times copper. And glass: a billion billion times, and beyond.

Few properties in all of physics span a range this wide. And it comes down to two things: how many electrons are free to move, and how easily they get through.

## 6. Temperature

**On screen:** The lattice warms from deep blue to orange and its ions shake harder; the electron paths become more tangled and the meter climbs. A filament at switch-on with a current trace that spikes and settles. A PT100 probe inside a generator winding. A silicon crystal that releases more carriers as it warms. Last, a lattice cooling until the electrons flow through untouched.

Resistivity is not fixed, though. It changes with temperature, and you already know why.

Heat a metal and its lattice trembles more violently. More trembling means more scattering, so the electrons are interrupted more often. Resistance goes up. For copper, it rises about four tenths of a percent for every degree Celsius.

A tungsten lamp filament shows this dramatically. Glowing at well over two thousand degrees, its resistance is more than ten times what it was when cold. That is why a filament bulb draws a surge of current at the instant you switch it on.

Engineers turn this effect into a thermometer. A platinum sensor called a PT100 has exactly one hundred ohms at zero degrees. Measure its resistance and you know its temperature. Generators and large motors have these buried in their windings.

Semiconductors such as silicon do the opposite. Heat shakes more electrons free, and that gain outweighs the extra scattering, so their resistance falls as they warm.

And cool certain materials far enough, and the resistance drops to exactly zero. That is superconductivity, and it is a story for another day.

## 7. Heat and the real world

**On screen:** A scattering event in slow motion: the electron's energy passes into the ion as a ripple that spreads through the lattice as a warm glow. *P = I²R* is written out. A heater coil, a toaster, a fuse wire melting. Then a transmission line across a landscape, with a split screen comparing low-voltage and high-voltage delivery of the same power and the heat lost in each.

One question is left. Every time an electron scatters, it loses the energy the field has just given it. Where does that energy go?

Into the lattice. Each scattering makes the atoms tremble a little harder, and trembling atoms are, quite literally, heat. So every conductor that carries a current warms up.

The power turned into heat is the current squared, times the resistance. P equals I squared R.

Sometimes that heat is the whole point. A heater, a toaster and a lamp filament are made from high-resistivity material so that they get hot on purpose. A fuse is a thin wire designed to melt when the current is too high, breaking the circuit before anything else is damaged.

But in a power line, that heat is pure loss. And notice the square. Halve the current and the loss falls to a quarter.

That is why electricity crosses the country at hundreds of thousands of volts. For the same power, ten times the voltage needs only one tenth of the current, and loses just one hundredth as much as heat.

It is also why every cable has a current rating. Push more through it than it was sized for, and the heat has nowhere to go.

---

## 8. Recap

**On screen:** The journey replays in half a minute, one image per sentence, and the formula assembles itself term by term. The camera pulls back out of the wire to the lamp, still lit.

So let's put it all together.

A metal is full of free electrons, always in motion. A voltage makes them drift. The disorder of the lattice keeps interrupting that drift, and that is resistance.

A longer path raises it. A wider path lowers it. The material sets the scale, through its resistivity. In metals, heat raises it further. And every interruption turns electrical energy into heat.

All of that, in one line: R equals rho, L over A.

Next time you meet that formula, don't just read it. See the wire.
