# Back and Forth: The Shape of Alternating Current

Narration script, draft 1. English, about 12 minutes. Series 2 (Alternating Current), video 1 of 6.

Lines in plain text are spoken. Lines marked **On screen** describe the picture and are not spoken. **Hero scene** marks a showpiece shot. A timeline strip runs along the bottom of the frame, as in the earlier videos.

---

## 1. The current that changes its mind

**On screen:** A wall socket, close up, in a quiet room. The view passes through the faceplate and into the copper behind it. Inside the wire, the cloud of electrons surges one way, stops, surges back, stops, again and again. A counter in the corner runs: reversals this second, climbing to one hundred. Then a lamp on the same circuit, glowing steadily.

Behind every wall socket there is a pair of wires. And in those wires, something odd is going on.

The current is not flowing out to your lamp and back, round and round, like water in a pipe. It flows one way. Then it stops. Then it flows the other way. Then it stops again. In most of the world, it changes direction a hundred times every second.

This is alternating current, or AC. It is the kind of electricity that every power station makes and every home receives.

It raises some fair questions. If the current keeps reversing, how does anything get done? If the voltage is always changing, what does it mean to say the supply is two hundred and thirty volts? And why did the world choose something this strange?

In this series we take AC apart, one idea at a time. We start with its shape.

## 2. Back and forth

**On screen:** Timeline: none yet. Inside the wire again, slowed down: the electrons shuffle a tiny distance one way, then the same distance back. A ruler shows the size of the shuffle: thousandths of a millimetre. Then a handsaw cutting a plank: the blade goes forward and back, and sawdust falls on both strokes. Then a long bicycle chain being rocked back and forth at one end, and a wheel at the far end rocking with it.

First, what are the electrons actually doing?

In the very first video, we saw that electrons in a wire drift astonishingly slowly, less than a millimetre a second. With alternating current they do not even get that far. They move a tiny distance one way, and then the same distance back. In an ordinary household wire, the whole journey is a few thousandths of a millimetre. The electrons in your lamp's cable have been in that cable since the day it was made.

So how does the energy arrive?

Think of a handsaw. The blade goes forward and back, forward and back. It never travels anywhere. But it cuts on every stroke, and the work gets done.

Or think of rocking a long chain back and forth at one end. The far end rocks too, at once. No link travels the length of the chain, and yet your effort arrives.

It is the same in the wire. The electrons at the power station push on their neighbours, which push on theirs, all the way to your lamp. What travels is the push. And a lamp filament does not care which way the electrons in it are moving. Either way they collide with the atoms, and the filament gets hot.

## 3. Reading the wave

**On screen:** Timeline: 1832. **Hero scene:** the turning loop from the last series, between its two magnet poles, drawn large. A bright point on the rim of the loop casts its height onto a ribbon that unrolls sideways like paper from a printer, tracing a smooth wave. One full turn of the loop draws one full wave on the ribbon. Labels appear on the wave: one cycle; the time it takes, the period; cycles in one second, the frequency. A stopwatch: twenty thousandths of a second.

To talk about alternating current, we draw it. Time runs along, and the voltage goes up and down.

You already know where this shape comes from. At the end of the last series we saw a generator: a loop of wire turning in a magnetic field. As it turns, the voltage rises to a peak, falls through zero, swings to a peak the other way, and comes back.

Watch one point on the turning loop, and plot its height as time goes by. Out comes the wave. One complete turn of the loop draws one complete wave. We call that one cycle.

Three words describe it.

The time for one cycle is the period.

The number of cycles in one second is the frequency. Its unit is the hertz, named after Heinrich Hertz, whom we met when he proved that radio waves exist. One hertz is one cycle each second.

In most of the world, the mains frequency is fifty hertz. Fifty cycles a second means each cycle lasts one fiftieth of a second: twenty thousandths. And because the current changes direction twice in each cycle, it reverses a hundred times a second.

Period and frequency are two ways of saying the same thing. Divide one into one, and you get the other.

## 4. Why this shape?

**On screen:** A gallery of wave shapes side by side: a square wave, a triangle, a sawtooth, and the smooth sine. Each is fed in turn through a coil and a capacitor: the square comes out with spikes, the triangle comes out rounded, the sawtooth is mangled. The sine goes in and comes out a sine, only shifted and resized. Then a pendulum, a weight bouncing on a spring, a child on a swing: each traces the same curve.

The curve has a name: a sine wave. And it is worth asking why the mains should have this particular shape. Why not a square wave, switching sharply from one direction to the other? Or a zigzag?

One reason we have seen. It is what you get from steady rotation, and generators rotate.

But there is a deeper reason, and it is why engineers love this curve.

Recall what a coil and a capacitor respond to. A coil produces a voltage that depends on how fast the current is changing. A capacitor passes a current that depends on how fast the voltage is changing. Both care about the rate of change.

Now take a sine wave and ask how fast it is changing at each moment. Plot the answer, and you get another sine wave. Same shape, same frequency, only shifted along.

No other repeating shape does this. Put a square wave into a circuit with coils and capacitors, and it comes out distorted, with spikes and rounded corners. Put a sine wave in, and a sine wave comes out. Its size may change, and it may be shifted in time. But its shape survives.

That is why a power system built on sine waves stays tidy from the generator to your socket.

It is also the natural shape of anything that swings. A pendulum, a weight on a spring, a child on a swing: plot any of them against time, and you get this same curve.

## 5. How big?

**On screen:** A sine wave with its height marked: the peak, also called the amplitude. A voltmeter beside a wall socket reads 230. The wave is then drawn to scale against that reading, and its crests rise well above the 230 line, to 325. A second socket, of the North American kind: 120 on the meter, 170 at the crest.

Now for size. The height of the wave, from zero to its crest, is called the peak value, or the amplitude.

Here is a surprise. Put a meter on a wall socket in Europe, India or Australia, and it reads about two hundred and thirty volts. But the voltage in that socket does not peak at two hundred and thirty. Twice in every cycle, it reaches three hundred and twenty-five.

In North America, the meter says one hundred and twenty, and the peak is one hundred and seventy.

So the number printed on every appliance is not the peak. It is not the average either, as we are about to see. It is a third thing. And to understand it, we have to ask what the number is for.

## 6. The number that matters

**On screen:** A sine wave with its average line drawn: exactly zero, the top half cancelling the bottom half. Then two identical electric heaters side by side, each warming a beaker of water with a thermometer. The left runs on a battery, a flat line on its graph. The right runs on AC. The battery voltage is turned up until both thermometers climb at exactly the same pace. Then the working: the wave is squared, and every part of it flips above the line; the squared wave is levelled out to its mean; a square root brings it back down. The result sits at 0.707 of the peak.

What single number should we use to describe a voltage that never stands still?

The obvious choice is the average. But look. The wave spends exactly as much time below zero as above it. Its average is zero. That cannot be right. A voltage whose average is zero still boils a kettle.

So try a different test. Do not ask what the voltage is. Ask what it can do.

Take two identical heaters. Run one from alternating current. Run the other from a steady, direct voltage, like a battery, and adjust that voltage until both heaters give out heat at exactly the same rate.

Whatever steady voltage does that is the fair measure of the alternating one. We call it the effective value.

We can work it out. The heat in a resistor depends on the voltage squared. So square the wave. Squaring makes every part of it positive, the dips as well as the crests, which is exactly right, because a heater warms up whichever way the current flows.

Now take the average, the mean, of that squared wave. Then take the square root, to undo the squaring.

Root, of the mean, of the square. That is where the name comes from: the R M S value.

For a sine wave, it always comes out the same way. The RMS value is the peak divided by the square root of two: about seventy percent of the peak.

So two hundred and thirty volts is an RMS value. It means: this supply heats a resistor exactly as a steady two hundred and thirty volts would. When an engineer quotes an alternating voltage or current, and says nothing else, RMS is what they mean.

But remember the peak is still there. Insulation has to withstand three hundred and twenty-five volts, not two hundred and thirty.

## 7. Fifty, or sixty?

**On screen:** Timeline: 1886, 1891, 1895, 1896. A map of the world in the 1890s with a scatter of early power stations, each labelled with a different frequency: 16⅔, 25, 40, 50, 60, 133. A lamp on a low-frequency supply, visibly flickering. A transformer on a high-frequency supply, shrinking. Then two companies' names: Westinghouse settling on sixty, AEG in Berlin on fifty. **Hero scene:** the map of Japan at night, lit in two colours along a line across the middle of the main island, fifty hertz to the east and sixty to the west.

Why fifty hertz? Or, if you live in the Americas, why sixty?

There is no deep law of nature behind it. In the early days, every company picked its own. There were systems at sixteen and two thirds, at twenty-five, forty, fifty, sixty, and a hundred and thirty-three cycles a second.

The choice is a compromise. Go too low, and lamps flicker visibly, and transformers have to be large and heavy. Go too high, and the losses in lines and machines climb. Somewhere between forty and sixty is comfortable.

In the United States, Westinghouse settled on sixty. In Germany, the firm of AEG chose fifty, a rounder number in a metric country. Each sold its equipment to its neighbours, and the world divided between them.

One country still shows the join. In eighteen ninety-five, Tokyo bought its generators from AEG, at fifty hertz. A year later, Osaka bought from General Electric, at sixty. Japan has run on two frequencies ever since, and the two halves of its grid can exchange power only through special converter stations.

Other choices survive where they suit the job. Some railways still use sixteen and two thirds. Aircraft use four hundred hertz, because at a high frequency transformers and motors can be made small and light, and on an aircraft every kilogram counts.

## 8. In step, and out of step

**On screen:** Two sine waves of the same frequency drawn one above the other, rising and falling together. One of them slides sideways: now it reaches its crest a little later. The gap between the crests is marked as an angle on the turning loop: a quarter turn, ninety degrees. Two loops fixed on one shaft at an angle to each other, each drawing its own wave on the ribbon. Then three.

There is one more thing a sine wave has, besides its size and its frequency. It has timing.

Take two waves of the same frequency. If they rise and fall together, we say they are in phase. But one may run a little behind the other, reaching its crest a moment later. Then they are out of phase.

We measure the gap not in seconds, but as a fraction of a turn. Think of the generator again. Fix two loops on the same shaft, at an angle to each other. They produce two waves, identical in shape, with one lagging the other by exactly that angle. A quarter of a turn is ninety degrees. Half a turn is a hundred and eighty, and then one wave is the mirror image of the other.

This idea of phase will turn out to be the key to everything that follows. It decides how much power a circuit really takes. It is the secret of the three-phase system that carries power across every country. And it starts with the simplest question in AC.

So let's put it together.

Alternating current flows one way and then the other. The electrons only shuffle back and forth, but the energy still arrives.

One complete swing is a cycle. The time it takes is the period, and the number of cycles each second is the frequency, in hertz.

The shape is a sine wave, because it comes from rotation, and because it is the one shape that coils and capacitors do not distort.

The height of the wave is its peak. The number on the label is the RMS value, the steady voltage that would give the same heat: about seventy percent of the peak.

And two waves of the same frequency can be in step, or out of step, by an angle we call phase.

Next time, we ask that simplest question. Put a resistor on an alternating supply, then a coil, then a capacitor. In two of the three, the current refuses to keep time with the voltage. We will see why.
