## 1. The snail in your wire

**On screen:** A copper wire standing upright, glowing. Inside it, a crowd of electrons jitters wildly, while the whole crowd creeps slowly upward. A snail beside it, keeping pace. A lamp at the top lights the instant a switch is pressed.

The electrons in your phone charger cable are slower than a snail.

Seriously. In a copper wire carrying a couple of amperes, the electrons drift along at less than a millimetre a second. A snail would overtake them.

So how does the lamp light the instant you flick the switch?

Because the electrons were already there. The wire is packed with them, from end to end. When you close the switch, a push travels down the wire almost at the speed of light, and every electron starts to shuffle at once. Including the ones already inside the lamp.

And as they shuffle, they keep crashing into the atoms of the metal. Every collision steals a little energy and turns it into heat. That is resistance.

Make the wire longer, and there are more atoms in the way. More resistance. Make it thicker, and there is more room to pass. Less resistance. Heat it up, and the atoms shake harder and get in the way more often.

That is why a heater gets hot, why a fuse melts, and why power lines are thick.

## 2. Rub a balloon, bend a stream of water

**On screen:** A plus charge and a minus charge floating in the dark, their field lines arching between them. One charge is pulled away and the force arrows shrink fast.

Rub a balloon on your hair, hold it near a thin stream of water, and the water bends towards it. Nothing touches it.

So what is pulling?

Electric charge. Rubbing moves a few electrons from your hair onto the balloon, and charge pushes or pulls on other charge across empty space. Like charges push apart. Opposite charges pull together.

And the force gets weaker fast. Twice as far away, and it is only a quarter as strong. Three times as far, a ninth. Charles Coulomb measured this in seventeen eighty-five with a twisted silver wire.

But how does one charge feel another across empty space? Michael Faraday's answer was the field. Every charge fills the space around it with a field, and anything that enters it feels a push. These glowing lines are a map of that field. They leave positive charges and end on negative ones. Where they crowd together, the push is strong.

That field is behind lightning, the screen you are reading, and every circuit you have ever used.

## 3. Two plates that never touch

**On screen:** Two metal plates facing each other across a gap, one filling with pink charge and the other with cyan. Field lines fill the gap. The gap narrows and more charge crowds on. A flash as a camera flash fires.

Your camera flash stores its energy between two pieces of metal that never touch.

This is a capacitor. Two plates, a thin gap between them, and nothing crossing that gap.

Connect a battery, and electrons are pulled off one plate and pushed onto the other. One plate turns positive, the other negative. And between them, an electric field builds up. That field is where the energy is kept.

How much charge can it hold? That is its capacitance. Bigger plates hold more. A smaller gap holds more, because each plate pulls harder on the other. Fill the gap with the right material, and it holds more again.

Then let it go all at once, and you get a burst of energy far quicker than any battery can give. That is your camera flash. And the same trick keeps your phone's power steady, starts ceiling fans, and tunes every radio.

The first capacitor was a glass jar, in seventeen forty-five. People called it lightning in a jar.

## 4. A compass needle twitched in 1820

**On screen:** A vertical wire with compass needles around it. Current switches on and the needles swing to form a circle. The current reverses and they all spin round.

In eighteen twenty, during a lecture, a compass needle twitched. And physics changed for good.

Hans Christian Oersted had a compass on the bench beside a wire. When he switched the current on, the needle swung. Switch it off, it swung back.

A current makes a magnetic field.

And look at its shape. The field does not point along the wire. It wraps around it in circles. Every compass around the wire turns to follow the circle. Reverse the current, and every needle spins round to point the other way.

Now wind that wire into a coil. All those circles add up inside it, and you get a magnet you can switch on and off. Put an iron core inside, and it gets hundreds of times stronger.

That is an electromagnet. It is in every motor, every speaker, every relay and every doorbell. And the field it makes is measured in teslas. Earth's field is about fifty millionths of a tesla.

## 5. Why switches spark

**On screen:** A coil wound with copper, a switch closing. The current climbs slowly as the magnetic field around the coil swells. The switch opens and a spark leaps across the gap.

Ever seen a spark when you pull a plug out? That spark is a coil refusing to let go.

A coil of wire does something strange. It hates any change in its current.

Switch it on, and the current does not jump to full. It climbs, slowly, because the coil is busy building a magnetic field, and building that field takes energy.

Now switch it off. The field collapses, and the coil gives that energy back. It tries to keep the current flowing. With the switch open, there is nowhere for it to go, so the voltage shoots up, sometimes to thousands of volts, until a spark jumps the gap.

This stubbornness is called inductance. It is measured in henries, after Joseph Henry, who found it in the eighteen thirties.

It can be a nuisance. It burns switch contacts. But it is also useful. The ignition coil in a petrol car uses that same jolt to fire the spark plugs, and coils smooth the current in almost every power supply.

## 6. Light is electricity and magnetism

**On screen:** A wave of light travelling through the dark: an electric field rising and falling in one direction, a magnetic field in another, each feeding the other. The wave stretches and shrinks from radio to X-rays.

Light is made of electricity and magnetism. Here is how we found out.

A changing magnetic field makes an electric field. Michael Faraday showed that in the eighteen thirties.

Then James Clerk Maxwell added the missing piece. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field too.

Put the two together, and something remarkable happens. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field, which changes, and makes an electric field, which changes, and so on. The pair can carry itself through empty space. A wave.

Maxwell worked out how fast that wave should travel. The answer was three hundred thousand kilometres a second. The speed of light. Light was this wave all along.

Twenty years later, Heinrich Hertz made these waves with a spark, and caught them across a room.

And they come in every size. Stretch them out, and you get radio and Wi-Fi. Squeeze them shorter, and you get infrared, visible light, ultraviolet and X-rays. All the same thing. Only the wavelength changes.

## 7. How a power station makes electricity

**On screen:** A coil of copper turning between the poles of a magnet. As it turns, a voltage wave grows beside it. Then two coils on one iron ring: a transformer.

Almost every bit of electricity you have ever used was made by spinning a magnet near a coil of wire.

In eighteen thirty-one, Michael Faraday pushed a magnet into a coil, and a current flowed. He pulled it out, and the current flowed the other way. Hold the magnet still, and nothing.

That is the secret. It is not the magnetism that makes the current. It is the change.

So keep it changing. Spin a coil between the poles of a magnet. As it turns, the field through it rises, falls and reverses, over and over. Out comes a voltage that swings back and forth. That is a generator, and that is exactly what a power station does, whether it burns coal, splits atoms or catches the wind.

The same idea gives us the transformer. Two coils on one iron ring. A changing current in one makes a changing field, and that makes a voltage in the other. More turns, more volts. That is how power stations send electricity hundreds of kilometres.

## 8. Your socket changes direction 100 times a second

**On screen:** A turning arrow drawing a sine wave. Lines mark 325 volts at the peak and 230 volts lower down. The wave flips above and below the line.

The current in your wall socket changes direction a hundred times every second.

This is alternating current. It flows one way, stops, flows the other way, and stops again. At fifty hertz, that is fifty complete back-and-forth cycles every second, which means a hundred reversals.

The electrons barely move. They just shuffle back and forth on the spot. But the push still reaches your lamp, the way a saw cuts on both strokes.

And here is a surprise. Your socket is called two hundred and thirty volts. But it actually peaks at three hundred and twenty-five, twice in every cycle.

So where does two hundred and thirty come from? It is the RMS value. It means this alternating supply heats things exactly as much as a steady two hundred and thirty volts would. It is the number that matters for doing work.

And why fifty hertz? In America it is sixty. Early companies each picked their own, and the world split between two of them. Japan still runs on both.

## 9. The current that runs late

**On screen:** Three components side by side, a resistor, a coil and a capacitor, each with its voltage wave in pink and current wave in cyan. Over the resistor the waves match. Over the coil, the current trails behind. Over the capacitor, it runs ahead.

Put a coil on an alternating supply, and the current arrives a quarter of a cycle late.

In a resistor, current and voltage keep perfect step. They peak together and cross zero together.

But a coil hates any change in current. So the current is always running behind the voltage. A quarter of a cycle behind. Ninety degrees.

A capacitor is the opposite. Its current is greatest when the voltage is changing fastest, so the current peaks first, a quarter of a cycle early.

Here is the trick to remember it: the word CIVIL. In a capacitor, C, current comes before voltage. I, then V. In an inductor, L, voltage comes before current. V, then I.

And this timing gap has a strange side effect. A coil or a capacitor can hold back the current without getting warm. It borrows energy for its field, then hands it straight back. Engineers call that reactance, and it is why loudspeakers can send the bass to one speaker and the treble to another.
