# The Invisible Push: Charge and the Electric Field

Narration script, draft 2 (history added). English, about 14 minutes. Video 1 of the six-part series (teaching order).

Lines in plain text are spoken. Lines marked **On screen** describe the picture and are not spoken. **Hero scene** marks a showpiece shot.

---

## 1. A force across empty space

**On screen:** A balloon rubbed on hair drifts to a wall and stays there. The camera pushes into the gap between balloon and wall: nothing in it. **Hero scene:** two people at arm's length dissolve into clouds of protons and electrons in perfect balance; one percent of extra electrons glows, and the Earth rises beneath them like a weight on a scale.

Rub a balloon on your hair and press it to a wall. It stays. Nothing is holding it. No glue, no string, no contact at all between the charges doing the work.

Something is reaching across empty space and pulling.

That something is the electric force, and it is astonishingly strong. Compare it with gravity for a single electron and a single proton: the electric pull is stronger by a thousand billion billion billion billion times.

So why don't we feel it everywhere? Because matter is balanced, almost perfectly. If you and a friend stood at arm's length, and each of you had just one percent more electrons than protons, the push between you would be enough to lift a weight equal to the entire Earth.

Every wire, every motor and every circuit you will ever study runs on this force. So let's see where it comes from.

---

## 2. Amber

**On screen:** A piece of golden amber rubbed with wool, lifting chaff and feathers, in warm lamplight. The Greek word *ēlektron* glows beside it. A timeline runs along the bottom of the frame and stays for the whole film: 600 BC, 1600, 1733, 1747. Gilbert's pivoting needle swinging toward rubbed glass. Du Fay's two gold leaves, one flying toward a rubbed rod and one fleeing it. Franklin's plus and minus signs written over two objects.

People noticed this force a very long time ago. Around six hundred BC, the Greek thinker Thales recorded that amber, rubbed with wool, would pull in feathers and bits of straw. The Greek word for amber is elektron. Every word we use today, electricity, electron, electronics, comes from a piece of fossil tree resin.

For two thousand years it stayed a curiosity. Then in the year sixteen hundred, William Gilbert, physician to Queen Elizabeth the First, tested hundreds of materials and found that glass, sulphur, wax and many gems did the same thing. He gave the effect its name: electric.

In seventeen thirty-three, the Frenchman Charles du Fay found something stranger. There were two kinds of electricity. Objects charged the same way pushed each other apart, and objects charged in different ways pulled together.

A few years later, Benjamin Franklin proposed that there was really one electrical substance, and that an object could have too much of it or too little. He called the two conditions positive and negative. We still use his names, and his choice of which was which.

## 3. Charge

**On screen:** Timeline: 1897, 1910. An atom: a nucleus of protons, a cloud of electrons, with matching counters showing zero net charge. Hair and balloon in close-up as electrons hop across; the counters tip, one positive, one negative, and their sum stays zero. Thomson's glowing cathode-ray tube. Millikan's oil drop hanging still between two plates, then a staircase of charge in steps of *e*. A lightning bolt beside a read-out of fifteen coulombs.

The source is a property of matter called charge. It is as basic as mass. Mass tells you how strongly something responds to gravity. Charge tells you how strongly it responds to the electric force.

But charge comes in two kinds, and that changes everything. We call them positive and negative. Protons carry the positive kind, electrons the negative kind, and the two amounts are exactly equal in size. Two charges of the same kind push each other apart. Two of opposite kinds pull together.

An ordinary atom has as many electrons as protons, so from outside it looks neutral. Charging an object does not create anything. It only moves electrons from one place to another. When you rub the balloon, electrons leave your hair and settle on the rubber. The balloon ends up negative, your hair ends up positive by the same amount, and the total has not changed. Charge is never made and never destroyed. It is only moved.

Franklin had to guess which way his electrical substance moved, and he guessed wrong. The thing that actually moves is the electron, discovered by J. J. Thomson in eighteen ninety-seven, and by Franklin's labels it is negative. That is why, to this day, the current we draw in a circuit points the opposite way to the electrons that carry it.

Charge also comes in whole steps. You can have one electron's worth of charge, or two, or a billion, but never half. Robert Millikan showed this around nineteen ten, by balancing tiny charged oil drops between two metal plates and finding that their charge always came in multiples of one smallest amount.

We measure charge in coulombs. One coulomb is the charge of about six billion billion electrons, and it is an enormous amount. A whole lightning bolt moves only about fifteen.

## 4. Coulomb's law

**On screen:** Timeline: 1785. Coulomb's torsion balance: a light rod hanging from a hair-thin wire inside a glass cylinder, a charged ball at one end, twisting as a second charged ball is brought near. Then two charged spheres on a ruler with a force arrow on each. The arrows grow as either charge is turned up. Then one sphere slides away: at twice the distance the arrows shrink to a quarter. **Hero scene:** a point of light radiating outward through nested transparent spheres, the same glow spread thinner over each larger shell. The formula assembles: *F = k q₁q₂ / r²*.

How strong is the force between two charges? In seventeen eighty-five, the French engineer Charles Coulomb found a way to measure it. He hung a light rod from a very fine wire, with a small charged ball on one end. When he brought a second charged ball close, the rod turned, and the twist in the wire told him the force. The forces were tiny, and his balance was sensitive enough to weigh them.

He found two simple rules.

First, the force grows with each charge. Double one charge and the force doubles. Double both and it is four times as strong.

Second, the force falls with distance, and it falls fast. Move the charges twice as far apart and the force drops to a quarter. Three times as far, a ninth. This is an inverse square law.

Here is why it has that shape. Picture the influence of a charge spreading outward evenly in every direction. At a distance r, it is spread over a sphere, and the area of a sphere grows with r squared. The same influence over four times the area is a quarter as strong.

Put the two rules together and you have Coulomb's law. The force equals a constant, times the first charge, times the second, divided by the distance squared.

That constant is huge: nine billion. Two charges of one coulomb each, a metre apart, would push on each other with the weight of nearly a million tonnes. That is why you never meet a loose coulomb.

And when there are more than two charges, nothing new is needed. Work out the force from each one separately, then add them as arrows.

## 5. The field

**On screen:** Timeline: 1830s, 1860s. Two distant charges; one is nudged and the other responds, with a question mark over the empty space between. Then the space fills in: around a single charge, an arrow at every point, long near the charge and short far away. A small test charge is carried around and its force arrow always matches the arrow already there. The test charge is removed and the arrows remain. *E = F / q*. Faraday at his bench, sketching curved lines between two charged bodies; the sketch lifts off the page and becomes the field.

Coulomb's law tells us how big the force is. It does not tell us how one charge can push on another without touching it. How does the second charge know the first one is there?

The answer is one of the most powerful ideas in all of physics. A charge does not act on other charges directly. It changes the space around itself. Every point in that space now carries a kind of instruction: if a charge were placed here, it would be pushed this hard, in this direction.

That condition of space is called the electric field.

The idea came from Michael Faraday, in the eighteen-thirties and forties. Faraday was a blacksmith's son who left school at thirteen to work for a bookbinder, and he never learned advanced mathematics. So he thought in pictures. Where others saw empty space between two charges, he saw it filled with lines of force. Twenty years later, James Clerk Maxwell turned Faraday's pictures into equations, and those equations still run the whole of electrical engineering.

To measure it, place a small positive test charge at a point and see what force it feels. Then divide that force by the size of the test charge. What is left belongs to the place, not to the tester. It is the force per unit of charge, measured in newtons per coulomb. We call it E.

Here is what textbooks rarely say plainly. The field is there whether or not anything is around to feel it. Take the test charge away, and the field remains, at every point, waiting.

Think of a weather map covered in wind arrows. The wind is there at every spot on the map, whether or not a kite happens to be flying. A charge placed in a field simply feels the arrow at the place where it sits. The force on it is its charge times the field: F equals q E.

For a single charge, the field points straight away from it if the charge is positive, and straight in toward it if negative. And it weakens with the square of the distance, exactly as Coulomb found.

## 6. Field lines

**On screen:** The arrows join up into smooth lines leaving a positive charge. A negative charge arrives and the lines bend across to it: the dipole. Two positive charges: the lines veer away from each other. Lines crowd where the field is strong and thin out where it is weak. A test charge placed between two lines still feels a force.

Arrows at every point quickly become a crowded picture. So we draw the field the way Faraday did, with lines.

The rules are simple. A field line begins on a positive charge and ends on a negative one. At every point, the direction of the line is the direction of the field. And where the lines are close together the field is strong, while where they spread apart it is weak.

Put a positive and a negative charge side by side, and the lines arc from one to the other. Put two positive charges side by side, and the lines swerve apart, as if the two patterns were pushing on each other.

Field lines never cross, because at any point the field can only point one way.

But remember what the lines are. They are a drawing, like the contour lines on a map of a hill. The field is not only on the lines. It fills all of the space between them as well.

## 7. Uniform fields and conductors

**On screen:** Two flat plates, one positive and one negative, with straight, evenly spaced lines between them. An electron released between the plates accelerates toward the positive one. Then a solid block of metal placed in the field: its free electrons shift, pile up on one face, and the lines inside vanish. Timeline: 1752, 1836. Faraday seated inside a room-sized cube covered in metal foil, an electroscope in his hands hanging limp, while long sparks jump from the outside of the cube. **Hero scene:** a car in a thunderstorm, a bolt striking it, the field lines flowing around the metal shell and the inside left dark and calm. A lightning rod with lines crowding at its tip; a faint violet corona along a transmission line at night.

One arrangement matters more than any other in electrical engineering. Take two flat metal plates, facing each other, one charged positive and one negative. Between them, the field lines run straight across, evenly spaced. The field has the same strength and the same direction everywhere in the gap. We call that a uniform field, and you will meet it again in the very next video, inside a capacitor.

Release an electron in that gap and it accelerates steadily toward the positive plate. It moves against the field arrows, because its charge is negative.

Now put a piece of metal into a field. A metal is full of electrons that are free to move, so they do. They shift until their own field exactly cancels the field inside the metal. In a fraction of a second, the field inside a conductor is zero, and any extra charge sits on its surface.

Faraday proved this in eighteen thirty-six in the most direct way possible. He built a wooden cube, twelve feet on each side, covered it in metal foil and wire, and sat inside it with his most sensitive instruments. His assistants charged the outside until sparks flew from its surface. Inside, he could detect nothing at all.

We still call such an enclosure a Faraday cage. It is why you are safe inside a car in a thunderstorm: the metal body carries the charge around you, and the field inside stays close to zero. Engineers call this shielding, and it is why sensitive cables are wrapped in a metal screen.

There is one more thing to notice. On a conductor, charge crowds onto sharp points and edges, and the field there becomes intense. Franklin put this to use. Having shown in seventeen fifty-two that lightning is electricity, he fitted buildings with a pointed metal rod wired to the ground, and the lightning rod has protected them ever since. A high-voltage line suffers from the same effect: the faint glow and the crackle around a conductor at night is called corona, and it is wasted energy.

## 8. Field and voltage

**On screen:** Timeline: 1800. Volta's pile: a stack of zinc and copper discs separated by brine-soaked card. A charge pushed uphill against the field arrows, with an energy bar rising as it goes. The two plates again with a battery across them: *E = V / d*, and the gap closing while the field read-out climbs. Air between two electrodes as the read-out passes three million volts per metre, then the spark.

Moving a charge against a field takes work, the same way that lifting a weight against gravity does. And that work is not lost. It is stored, ready to be given back.

The work needed for each coulomb of charge is called the potential difference. You already know it by another name: voltage. The unit honours Alessandro Volta, who in eighteen hundred stacked discs of zinc and copper into the first battery, and gave the world its first steady source of electrical push. One volt means one joule of work for each coulomb moved.

Between our two plates, voltage and field are tied together very simply. The field equals the voltage divided by the gap: E equals V over d. So the field can also be measured in volts per metre. Put one volt across a one-millimetre gap, and the field is a thousand volts per metre.

Squeeze the same voltage into a smaller gap and the field grows. And every insulator has a limit. For air, it is about three million volts per metre. Push past that, and the air itself is torn apart and starts to conduct. That is a spark. On a much larger scale, that is lightning.

This is why insulation thickness, spacing between conductors and the smooth shape of high-voltage equipment are never arbitrary. They are all ways of keeping the field below the limit.

## 9. Recap

**On screen:** The timeline, now complete from 600 BC to 1910, with each name lit. The balloon on the wall again, now with the field drawn in around it. Each idea returns as one image: the two kinds of charge, the shrinking force arrows, the arrows filling space, the lines, the plates. The final frame holds *F = qE* over the field.

So let's put it together.

It began with a piece of amber, and it took twenty-five centuries to understand.

Charge comes in two kinds. Like charges repel, opposite charges attract, and charge is never created or destroyed, only moved.

The force between two charges grows with each charge and falls with the square of the distance.

A charge fills the space around it with an electric field: the force that each unit of charge would feel at every point.

We draw that field with lines, from positive to negative, crowded where it is strong.

Inside a conductor the field is zero. Between two plates it is uniform, and equal to the voltage divided by the gap.

Next time, we put those two plates to work, and store energy in the empty space between them.
