# Tipu Sultan: Tyrant or Tiger?

Narration script, draft 2, for the channel **Hemanth Explains**. English, about 13 minutes (2,006 spoken words). Not yet approved.

Lines in plain text are spoken. Lines marked **On screen** describe the picture and are not spoken.

Principles for this film:
- Every factual claim is one that mainstream historians accept, and the script says where it comes from.
- Where historians agree an event happened but disagree about its scale, the script says so and gives the range, not a single dramatic number.
- The history chapters stay even-handed. The channel's own view comes in chapter 8: the history deserves to be studied, but a state-sponsored celebration of so divisive a figure looked more like political calculation than remembrance. That view is stated as the channel's opinion, argued from evidence, and aimed at governments and politicians, not at any community.
- No inflammatory images: no blood, no burning temples or mosques, no caricatures of any community. Visuals are maps, the fort, documents, objects, and the timeline.

## 1. A birthday that divides a state

**On screen:** A dark map of Karnataka. A single date appears: 10 November. Newspaper-style headlines in neutral type fade in and out, one supporting the celebration, one opposing it. A timeline strip begins at the bottom: 2015, 2019.

Every November, one birthday divides Karnataka.

It belongs to a ruler who died more than two hundred years ago. Tipu Sultan, the king of Mysore.

In twenty fifteen, the state government began celebrating his birth anniversary officially, as Tipu Jayanti. The celebrations brought protests. In Kodagu, the protests turned violent, and a man died. In twenty nineteen, a new government cancelled the official celebration. And every year since, the argument has returned.

One side says Tipu was a tyrant. That he persecuted Hindus and Christians, destroyed temples and forced people to convert, and that no government should honour him.

The other side says he was a patriot. A modernising king who fought the British East India Company to the death, decades before anyone else in India thought of it, and who protected temples too.

Both sides quote history. So in this video, we go to the history itself. What do we actually know about Tipu Sultan? Where does that knowledge come from? And at the end, I will tell you what I think of the celebration itself, and why.

## 2. Who was Tipu?

**On screen:** A map of south India in the 1760s. The kingdom of Mysore highlighted, small at first, then spreading under Hyder Ali. Portrait silhouettes, drawn in line, of Hyder Ali and the young Tipu. The timeline adds 1750, 1761, 1782.

Tipu was born in seventeen fifty, at Devanahalli, near today's Bengaluru airport.

His father, Hyder Ali, was a soldier of humble origins who rose through the army of the Wodeyar kings of Mysore. By the seventeen sixties, Hyder was the real power in the kingdom. The Wodeyars remained on the throne in name, but Hyder ruled.

Hyder could not read or write. He made sure his son could. Tipu was educated in languages, religion and mathematics, and trained as a soldier from boyhood. He was leading cavalry in battle as a teenager.

When Hyder Ali died in seventeen eighty-two, in the middle of a war with the British, Tipu took his place. He ruled for seventeen years, from his island capital of Srirangapatna, on the Kaveri.

And for almost all of those seventeen years, he was at war.

## 3. The Tiger who fought the Company

**On screen:** The map widens to all of India in the 1780s. The East India Company's territories in red, spreading from Bengal and Madras. Mysore holds the south. Battle markers for the four Anglo-Mysore wars appear on the timeline: 1767, 1780, 1790, 1799. **Hero scene:** a 3D Mysorean rocket, an iron tube on a bamboo pole, launches in slow motion with its arc drawn across the sky. Then the rocket in a museum case. Then Tipu's Tiger, the wooden automaton, in a museum case.

To understand why Tipu matters to so many people, look at what was happening to India.

In the seventeen sixties, the East India Company was swallowing the country, kingdom by kingdom. Mysore was one of the few powers that fought it, and won.

Mysore and the Company fought four wars. In the second, in seventeen eighty, at a place called Pollilur, Hyder and Tipu destroyed an entire British force. It was one of the worst defeats the Company ever suffered in India.

Tipu's armies used a weapon the British had never faced. Rockets with iron casings, launched in their hundreds. The British were so impressed that, after Tipu's defeat, they shipped captured rockets to England. An engineer named William Congreve studied them and developed the Congreve rocket, which the British army went on to use in Europe and America. Some of Tipu's rockets are still in British museums.

But the Company had more money, more men, and allies among Tipu's Indian neighbours, the Marathas and the Nizam of Hyderabad. In seventeen ninety-two, Tipu was forced to give up half his kingdom, and to hand over two of his young sons as hostages.

In seventeen ninety-nine, the British stormed Srirangapatna. Tipu died fighting at the gates of his own fort. He had refused to surrender.

After his death, the British carried away his treasures. One of them, a life-size wooden tiger mauling a European soldier, with a hidden organ that makes it roar, sits today in a museum in London. Tipu's symbol was the tiger, and he is still called the Tiger of Mysore.

## 4. The moderniser

**On screen:** A set of objects, drawn in line and gold: a coin, a calendar page, a silkworm cocoon, a merchant ship. Then a map with lines to Paris, Constantinople and Kabul for Tipu's envoys. A ledger with the name Purnaiah.

Tipu was not only a soldier. Historians across the spectrum agree he was an unusually energetic administrator.

He reorganised land revenue, so that tax was collected directly by the state rather than through middlemen. He issued new coins, introduced new weights and measures, and even his own calendar.

He set up state trading posts, and tried to build a navy. He encouraged new crafts and crops. The silk industry of the Mysore region is often traced back to his efforts to bring in silkworms.

He sent envoys abroad, to the king of France and to the Ottoman Sultan in Constantinople, looking for allies against the British.

And his government employed Hindus at the highest levels. His chief finance minister, Purnaiah, was a Hindu Brahmin who served both Hyder and Tipu, and went on to serve the Wodeyars after them.

This is the Tipu his admirers celebrate. A king who saw the Company coming, fought it to the end, and tried to build a modern state.

## 5. The case against

**On screen:** The map of Tipu's kingdom, now showing three regions in turn: Kodagu, the Malabar coast, and coastal Karnataka around Mangaluru. Each is marked with a date range on the timeline. Quotations appear as plain text on aged paper, each labelled with its source. Numbers appear only as ranges.

Now the other Tipu. And this is not invented. Much of the evidence comes from Tipu's own letters.

In Kodagu, the Kodavas rose against Mysore rule again and again in the seventeen eighties. Tipu's response was brutal. Large numbers of Kodavas were deported to Srirangapatna, and many were forcibly converted to Islam. Tipu's own letters boast about it. Historians disagree on the numbers, and some think Tipu's figures were inflated to impress. But that it happened is not seriously disputed.

On the Malabar coast, in today's Kerala, Tipu's campaigns around seventeen eighty-nine brought forced conversions, especially of Nairs, and the destruction of temples. Again, his letters describe them. Thousands of Hindu families fled south to Travancore.

And on the coast around Mangaluru, Tipu suspected the Catholic community of helping the British. In seventeen eighty-four, he had tens of thousands of Mangalorean Catholics marched to Srirangapatna. Estimates range from about thirty thousand to sixty thousand people. Many died on the march or in captivity, and many were forcibly converted. The community still remembers it as the captivity at Seringapatam.

These events are why many people in Kodagu, on the coast and in Kerala do not see Tipu as a hero. For them, this is not abstract history. It is what happened to their ancestors.

## 6. The case for

**On screen:** A map of temple locations within Tipu's kingdom, lit one by one: Sringeri, Srirangapatna, Melkote, Nanjangud. A letter in Persian appears with its English translation beneath, and a date: 1791. The Ranganathaswamy temple and Tipu's palace shown side by side on a plan of Srirangapatna.

But the same Tipu also did this.

In seventeen ninety-one, a Maratha army raided the Sringeri math, one of the most important centres of Hindu learning, and desecrated the temple. Tipu wrote to the Shankaracharya expressing his anger, and sent money and grain to restore the goddess Sharada. About thirty of his letters to Sringeri survive. They were found in nineteen sixteen and published by the Mysore archaeology department. In them, Tipu asks the Swami to pray for his kingdom.

He made grants to other temples, including those at Melkote and Nanjangud. The great Ranganathaswamy temple at Srirangapatna stood a short walk from his own palace, and it was never harmed.

So how can the same ruler be both?

Many historians argue that Tipu's violence followed politics and war more than faith alone. In his core territory, where he needed his subjects' loyalty, he patronised temples. Where communities rebelled, or where he suspected them of siding with the British, as in Kodagu, Malabar and among the coastal Catholics, he was ruthless, and religion became part of the punishment.

Other historians answer that this does not make it better. Forced conversion is religious persecution, whatever the motive. And Tipu's own words, calling some of these campaigns holy war, show that religion mattered to him.

Both of those are honest readings of the evidence.

## 7. Who wrote the history?

**On screen:** Three bookshelves appear one after another, each labelled with a period: "Written by the victors, 1800s", "After independence, 1950s onward", "Today". Book spines show the authors named in the narration. The timeline adds 1810 and 1951.

There is one more thing to understand. The way we see Tipu has always depended on who was writing.

The first histories of Tipu were written by the British who defeated him. Officers like Mark Wilks and William Kirkpatrick described him as a cruel fanatic. They had good reason to. A tyrant overthrown makes a conquest look like a rescue.

After independence, many Indian historians, such as Mohibbul Hasan, looked again. They saw a patriot and a moderniser, and argued that the British had exaggerated his cruelty.

More recently, other writers have returned to the persecution, using Tipu's own letters and the memories of the communities who suffered.

And some historians, like Kate Brittlebank, point out that Tipu was trying to be accepted as a legitimate king in a land where he was a newcomer, and used both Islamic and Hindu symbols to do it.

Each of these historians uses real sources. They differ in which sources they put first, and in what question they are asking.

## 8. How to hold both

**On screen:** A balance appears, with two pans. Each piece of evidence from chapters 3 to 6 drops into one pan or the other, as a small labelled card. The balance does not settle. Then the cards rearrange into a single timeline of one life.

So, was Tipu a tyrant or a hero?

Here is a way to think about it.

First, judge him by his own time, but do not excuse him by it. Eighteenth-century India was brutal on every side. The Maratha army that sacked Sringeri was Hindu. The East India Company plundered and starved whole regions. Tipu was not unusual in his violence. But that does not make what happened in Kodagu, Malabar or Mangaluru any less real to the people it happened to.

Second, separate the questions. Did Tipu fight colonial rule, and modernise his state? The evidence says yes. Did he persecute communities, and force conversions? The evidence says yes to that too. Both are true at once.

Third, notice that the modern argument is not really about seventeen ninety-nine. It is about today.

**On screen:** A calendar of Karnataka's official jayantis fills the screen, one tile per figure, each tagged with the community that claims them. The Tipu Jayanti tile is highlighted. Then a ballot box.

And here is where I will give you my own view.

Karnataka celebrates many jayantis. Over the years, governments of every party have added official birthdays, very often for a figure whom a particular community holds dear. A jayanti has become a way to tell a community: we see you. And, at election time, to ask for its votes.

Tipu Jayanti fits that pattern. Several Muslim organisations and leaders had long championed Tipu as a community icon, one of the few Muslim rulers in Karnataka's past remembered as a fighter against the British. And for many Muslims in the state, he became a symbol of pride. Politicians saw that, and turned it into an official celebration.

To its critics, that was appeasement. A state honouring one community's hero, without asking how it would feel to the Kodavas, the coastal Catholics, or the Hindu families whose ancestors suffered under him. And it was rushed into being with no attempt at a shared understanding of the history. The result was not remembrance. It was protest, division, and in Kodagu, a death.

I think that criticism is fair. Not because Tipu is unworthy of being studied, or because his fight against the Company did not matter. But because a government should not pick a side in a wound that is still open, and should not use history to collect votes. If a state wants to remember Tipu, it should remember all of him: the rockets and the resistance, and Kodagu, Malabar and Mangaluru too.

That is a debate about the present. And it is better argued with the whole of the history, not half of it.

Studying a historical figure, remembering him, and worshipping him are three different things. You can study Tipu without worshipping him. And you can criticise him without erasing what he did against the Company.

## 9. Recap

**On screen:** The timeline, complete. The key images return: the rocket, the coin, the map of the three regions, the Sringeri letter, the three bookshelves. End card: Like · Share · Subscribe, with the channel name Hemanth Explains.

So let's put it together.

Tipu Sultan ruled Mysore from seventeen eighty-two to seventeen ninety-nine. He fought the East India Company in four wars, used rockets that the British later copied, and died fighting at Srirangapatna.

He was an active, modernising ruler, and he employed Hindus at the highest level of his government.

He also carried out brutal campaigns in Kodagu, Malabar and against the Catholics of the coast, including deportations and forced conversions. His own letters record them.

And he protected and funded temples, including Sringeri, in other parts of his kingdom.

Our picture of him has been shaped by who was writing: the British who defeated him, the nationalists who reclaimed him, and the communities who remember what he did to them.

And the official celebration of his birthday has more to do with today's politics than with the eighteenth century.

History does not have to give us heroes or villains. It gives us people, and the evidence of what they did. What we celebrate is our choice. It should be an informed one, and it should not be made for us by whoever needs our votes.

## Sources to check before recording

Claims in the script rest on these. Before voicing, a quick check against them is recommended, especially the dates and the ranges of numbers.

- Mohibbul Hasan, *History of Tipu Sultan* (1951): the nationalist reassessment; administration, wars, Sringeri.
- Kate Brittlebank, *Tipu Sultan's Search for Legitimacy* (1997), and *Tiger: The Life of Tipu Sultan* (2016): legitimacy, the use of Hindu and Islamic symbols.
- Irfan Habib (ed.), *Confronting Colonialism: Resistance and Modernization under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan* (1999): administration and economy.
- Vikram Sampath, *Tipu Sultan: The Saga of the Mysore Interregnum* (2024): a critical account emphasising the persecution, drawing on Tipu's letters.
- William Kirkpatrick (trans.), *Select Letters of Tippoo Sultan* (1811), and Mark Wilks, *Historical Sketches of the South of India* (1810–1817): the early British accounts; Tipu's own letters.
- Mysore Archaeological Department, *Annual Report* (1916): the Sringeri letters.
- On the Mangalorean Catholic captivity: Severine Silva, *History of Christianity in Canara* (1957), and the community's own histories; estimates of the numbers vary widely.
- On Tipu Jayanti politics: newspaper reporting from 2015 (state celebration begins; protests and a death in Kodagu) and 2019 (celebration cancelled), including the appeasement criticism made at the time and the responses to it. Check the list of Karnataka's official jayantis before the calendar graphic is made.
- The rockets: Royal Artillery Museum collections; William Congreve's own account of his rocket (1807).
- Tipu's Tiger: Victoria and Albert Museum, London.
