# Tipu Sultan: Tyrant or Tiger?

Narration script, draft 3, for the channel **Hemanth Explains**. English, about 14 minutes. Not yet approved.

Draft 3 is corrected against the Gemini Deep Research fact-check ("Tipu Sultan and Jayanti Verification", November 2026). See the fact-check notes at the end for what changed and what still needs checking.

Lines in plain text are spoken. Lines marked **On screen** describe the picture and are not spoken.

Principles for this film:
- Every factual claim is one that the fact-check confirmed, or is worded to match the level of evidence: "documented", "according to British accounts", "letters attributed to Tipu", "estimates range from…".
- Where historians agree an event happened but disagree about its scale, the script gives the range and who gives it, not a single dramatic number.
- The history chapters stay even-handed. The channel's own view comes in chapter 8: the history deserves to be studied, but a state-sponsored celebration of so divisive a figure looked more like political calculation than remembrance. That view is stated as the channel's opinion, argued from evidence, and aimed at governments and politicians, not at any community.
- No inflammatory images: no blood, no burning temples or churches, no caricatures of any community. Visuals are maps, the fort, documents, objects, and the timeline.

## 1. A birthday that divides a state

**On screen:** A dark map of Karnataka. A single date appears: 10 November. Two quotations fade in on either side, each with name and date: Siddaramaiah, 2019, "lover of freedom, freedom fighter"; BJP Karnataka, 2019, "controversial and communal". A timeline strip begins at the bottom: 2015, 2019.

Every November, one birthday divides Karnataka.

It belongs to a ruler who died more than two hundred years ago. Tipu Sultan, the king of Mysore.

In twenty fifteen, Siddaramaiah's Congress government began celebrating his birth anniversary officially, as Tipu Jayanti. The celebrations brought protests. In Madikeri, in Kodagu, the protests turned violent, and two men lost their lives.

In twenty nineteen, B. S. Yediyurappa's new BJP government cancelled the official celebration, calling it controversial and communal. Siddaramaiah answered that Tipu had been honoured as a lover of freedom and a freedom fighter, and that the cancellation came from hatred of minorities. And every year since, the argument has returned.

One side says Tipu was a tyrant. That he persecuted Hindus and Christians, destroyed temples and forced people to convert, and that no government should honour him.

The other side says he was a patriot. A modernising king who fought the British East India Company to the death, and who protected temples too.

Both sides quote history. So in this video, we go to the history itself. What do we actually know about Tipu Sultan? Where does that knowledge come from? And at the end, I will tell you what I think of the celebration itself, and why.

## 2. Who was Tipu?

**On screen:** A map of south India in the 1760s. The kingdom of Mysore highlighted, small at first, then spreading under Hyder Ali. Portrait silhouettes, drawn in line, of Hyder Ali and the young Tipu. The timeline adds 1750, 1761, 1782.

Tipu was born at Devanahalli, near today's Bengaluru airport. Most historians date his birth to November seventeen fifty.

His father, Hyder Ali, rose through the army of the Wodeyar kings of Mysore. By seventeen sixty-one, Hyder was the real power in the kingdom. The Wodeyars remained on the throne in name, but Hyder ruled.

Hyder had no formal schooling. He made sure his son did. Tipu learned Persian and Arabic, religion and law, and the military methods of the French officers who served Mysore. In seventeen sixty-seven, still a teenager, he led a cavalry raid deep into enemy territory.

When Hyder Ali died in December seventeen eighty-two, in the middle of a war with the British, Tipu took his place. He ruled for seventeen years, from his island capital of Srirangapatna, on the Kaveri.

And for almost all of those seventeen years, he was at war.

## 3. The Tiger who fought the Company

**On screen:** The map widens to all of India in the 1780s. The East India Company's territories in red, spreading from Bengal and Madras. Mysore holds the south. The four Anglo-Mysore wars appear on the timeline: 1767, 1780, 1790, 1799. **Hero scene:** a 3D Mysorean rocket, an iron tube on a bamboo pole, launches in slow motion with its arc drawn across the sky. Then rockets in a museum store. Then Tipu's Tiger, the wooden automaton, in a museum case.

To understand why Tipu matters to so many people, look at what was happening to India.

In the seventeen sixties, the East India Company was swallowing the country, kingdom by kingdom. Mysore was one of the few powers that fought it, and won.

Mysore and the Company fought four wars. In the second, in September seventeen eighty, at a place called Pollilur, Hyder and Tipu destroyed a British force under Colonel Baillie. A British officer, Thomas Munro, called it the severest blow that the English ever sustained in India.

Mysore's armies used a weapon the British had never faced. Rockets with iron casings, launched in their hundreds. After Tipu's defeat, captured rockets were taken to the Royal Arsenal at Woolwich, in England. There, an engineer named William Congreve studied them and developed the Congreve rocket, which the British army went on to use in Europe and America.

But the Company had more money, more men, and allies among Tipu's Indian neighbours. In the third war, the Marathas and the Nizam of Hyderabad fought alongside the British. In seventeen ninety-two, Tipu was forced to give up about half his kingdom, and to hand over two of his young sons as hostages.

In seventeen ninety-nine, the British stormed Srirangapatna. On the fourth of May, Tipu refused to surrender, and died fighting at a gateway of his own fort.

After his death, the British carried away his treasures. One of them, a wooden tiger mauling a European soldier, with a hidden organ that makes it roar, is in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London. Tipu's symbol was the tiger, and he is still called the Tiger of Mysore.

## 4. The moderniser

**On screen:** A set of objects, drawn in line and gold: a coin, a page of the Mauludi calendar, a silkworm cocoon. Then a map with lines to France and Constantinople for Tipu's envoys. A ledger with the name Purnaiah.

Tipu was not only a soldier. He was an energetic administrator.

In seventeen eighty-four he introduced his own calendar, the Mauludi calendar, which began each year at Ugadi and was designed to line up the collection of taxes with the harvest. He issued his own coins, and reorganised how land revenue was collected.

The silk industry of the Mysore region is often credited to his efforts to bring in silkworms, though the documentary evidence for that is thin.

He sent envoys abroad, to France and to the Ottoman Sultan, looking for allies against the British.

And his government employed Hindus at high levels. The most famous was Purnaiah, who managed the kingdom's finances under Tipu, and after Tipu's death became Diwan of Mysore under the restored Wodeyars.

This is the Tipu his admirers celebrate. A king who saw the Company coming, fought it to the end, and tried to build a modern state.

## 5. The case against

**On screen:** The map of Tipu's kingdom, now showing three regions in turn: Kodagu, the Malabar coast, and coastal Karnataka around Mangaluru. Each is marked with a date range on the timeline. Quotations appear as plain text on aged paper, each labelled "attributed to Tipu" with its source. Numbers appear only as ranges, each with the name of who gave it.

Now the other Tipu. And this is not invented.

In Kodagu, the Kodavas rose against Mysore rule again and again in the seventeen eighties. Tipu put down the risings with great force, and is accused of deporting Kodavas to Srirangapatna and forcing many to convert to Islam. These accusations go back to his own time. How many people were affected is disputed, and historians do not agree on the numbers.

On the Malabar coast, in today's Kerala, Tipu's campaigns between seventeen eighty-eight and seventeen ninety brought forced conversions and attacks on temples. Letters attributed to Tipu boast of converting huge numbers of Hindus there. One, dated January seventeen ninety, claims more than four lakh. But historians caution that these letters reach us through translations, some with conflicting dates, and that a ruler's boast is not a count.

On the coast around Mangaluru, Tipu suspected the Catholic community of helping the British. From February seventeen eighty-four, he had the community deported to Srirangapatna. Thomas Munro, the first British collector of the region, put the number taken at about sixty thousand; other estimates go as high as eighty thousand. Many died on the march or in captivity, and many were forcibly converted. When the captivity ended in seventeen ninety-nine, estimates of how many returned as Christians range from about eleven thousand to twenty thousand. The community still remembers it as the captivity at Seringapatam.

These events are why many people in Kodagu, on the coast and in Kerala do not see Tipu as a hero. For them, this is not abstract history. It is what happened to their ancestors.

## 6. The case for

**On screen:** The Sringeri math on a map of Tipu's kingdom. A letter appears with its English translation beneath, and a date: 1791. Then a plan of Srirangapatna, with the Ranganathaswamy temple and Tipu's palace side by side.

But the same Tipu also did this.

In seventeen ninety-one, during the war, Maratha troops raided the Sringeri math, one of the most important centres of Hindu learning. Tipu wrote to the Shankaracharya condemning the attack, sent gifts for the goddess Sharada, and ordered his officials to help the math. Forty-seven of his letters to Sringeri survive. They were found in nineteen sixteen by the Mysore archaeology department. In them, Tipu writes to the Swami with respect, and asks for his blessings.

Historians agree that Tipu also gave patronage to other Hindu temples in his kingdom. The great Ranganathaswamy temple at Srirangapatna stood a short walk from his own palace, and it stood through his reign.

So how can the same ruler be both?

Many historians argue that Tipu's violence followed politics and war more than faith alone. In his core territory, where he needed his subjects' loyalty, he patronised temples. Where communities rebelled, or where he suspected them of siding with the British, as in Kodagu, Malabar and among the coastal Catholics, he was ruthless, and religion became part of the punishment.

Other historians answer that this does not make it better. Forced conversion is religious persecution, whatever the motive. And letters attributed to Tipu describe some of these campaigns as a holy war, which suggests religion mattered to him.

Both of those are honest readings of the evidence.

## 7. Who wrote the history?

**On screen:** Three bookshelves appear one after another, each labelled with a period: "Written by the victors, 1800s", "After independence, 1950s onward", "Today". Book spines show the authors named in the narration. The timeline adds 1811, 1951 and 2024.

There is one more thing to understand. The way we see Tipu has always depended on who was writing.

The first histories of Tipu were written by the British who defeated him. Officers like Mark Wilks portrayed him as a cruel, fanatical tyrant, and William Kirkpatrick published translations of his letters. They had reason to. A tyrant overthrown makes a conquest look like a rescue.

After independence, Indian historians such as Mohibbul Hasan looked again. They saw a patriot and a moderniser, and argued that the British had exaggerated his cruelty.

More recently, writers such as Vikram Sampath have returned to the persecution, while still crediting his reforms. One reviewer summed up Sampath's Tipu as a ruler of forward-looking policies and backward-looking fundamentalism.

And historians like Kate Brittlebank point out that Tipu's family had taken power from the Wodeyars, so Tipu had to work hard to be accepted as a legitimate king, and used both Islamic and Hindu ideas of kingship to do it.

Each of these historians uses real sources. They differ in which sources they put first, and in what question they are asking.

## 8. How to hold both

**On screen:** A balance appears, with two pans. Each piece of evidence from chapters 3 to 6 drops into one pan or the other, as a small labelled card. The balance does not settle. Then the cards rearrange into a single timeline of one life.

So, was Tipu a tyrant or a hero?

Here is a way to think about it.

First, judge him by his own time, but do not excuse him by it. Eighteenth-century India was brutal on every side. The army that raided Sringeri was Maratha. The East India Company plundered whole regions. Tipu was not unusual in his violence. But that does not make what happened in Kodagu, Malabar or Mangaluru any less real to the people it happened to.

Second, separate the questions. Did Tipu fight colonial rule, and modernise his state? The evidence says yes. Did he persecute communities, and force conversions? The evidence says yes to that too. Both are true at once.

Third, notice that the modern argument is not really about seventeen ninety-nine. It is about today.

**On screen:** A calendar of Karnataka's official jayantis fills the screen, one tile per figure. The Tipu Jayanti tile is highlighted. Then a ballot box.

And here is where I will give you my own view.

Karnataka observes many official jayantis, and critics across parties have long said that some of them are chosen with one eye on community votes. A jayanti tells a community: we see you. And, at election time, it asks for its votes.

For many Muslims in Karnataka, Tipu is a source of pride, one of the few rulers from the state's past who is remembered as a fighter against the British. That feeling is real, and it deserves respect.

But critics argued that the government of the day turned that feeling into an official celebration in order to win votes. To them, it was appeasement: a state honouring one community's hero, without asking how it would feel to the Kodavas, the coastal Catholics, or the Hindu families whose ancestors suffered under him. And it was rushed into being with no attempt at a shared understanding of the history. The result was not remembrance. It was protest, division, and in Madikeri, two lives lost.

I think that criticism is fair. Not because Tipu is unworthy of being studied, or because his fight against the Company did not matter. But because a government should not pick a side in a wound that is still open, and should not use history to collect votes. If a state wants to remember Tipu, it should remember all of him: the rockets and the resistance, and Kodagu, Malabar and Mangaluru too.

That is a debate about the present. And it is better argued with the whole of the history, not half of it.

Studying a historical figure, remembering him, and worshipping him are three different things. You can study Tipu without worshipping him. And you can criticise him without erasing what he did against the Company.

## 9. Recap

**On screen:** The timeline, complete. The key images return: the rocket, the coin, the map of the three regions, the Sringeri letter, the three bookshelves. End card: Like · Share · Subscribe, with the channel name Hemanth Explains.

So let's put it together.

Tipu Sultan ruled Mysore from seventeen eighty-two to seventeen ninety-nine. He fought the East India Company in four wars, used rockets that the British later copied, and died fighting at Srirangapatna.

He was an active, modernising ruler, and he employed Hindus at high levels of his government.

He also carried out brutal campaigns in Kodagu, Malabar and against the Catholics of the coast, including deportations and forced conversions, though historians argue over their scale.

And he protected and supported temples, including Sringeri, in other parts of his kingdom.

Our picture of him has been shaped by who was writing: the British who defeated him, the historians who reclaimed him, and the communities who remember what he did to them.

And the official celebration of his birthday has more to do with today's politics than with the eighteenth century.

History does not have to give us heroes or villains. It gives us people, and the evidence of what they did. What we celebrate is our choice. It should be an informed one, and it should not be made for us by whoever needs our votes.
