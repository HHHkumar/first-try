# The Field That Moves the World: Electromagnetic Induction

## 1. The autumn of 1831

**On screen:** Timeline: 1831. A lit city at night, seen from above. The view falls back along a transmission line, through a substation, into a power station, and into the heart of one generator: a magnet turning inside coils of wire. Then a dissolve to a small basement laboratory in London, a coil, a magnet, and a needle.

Look at a city at night. Every lamp, every screen, every motor in it is running on electricity that was made a moment ago, somewhere far away.

Follow the wires back. Through the substation, along the transmission line, into the power station. Whether it burns coal or gas, splits atoms, or stands under a dam or a wind turbine, at its centre you find the same thing. A magnet, turning, inside coils of wire.

That is all. Nearly every unit of electricity you have ever used was made by moving a magnet near a wire.

The principle was found in a basement laboratory in London, over about ten weeks in the autumn of eighteen thirty-one, by Michael Faraday. It is called electromagnetic induction. In this last video of the series, we take it apart properly.

## 2. Flux

**On screen:** A wire loop in a uniform magnetic field, with the field lines passing through it counted. The field grows stronger and the count rises. The loop grows and the count rises. The loop tilts, and fewer lines pass through, until edge-on there are none. Beside it, a window frame in the rain, tilted the same way. *Φ = B × A*. Wilhelm Weber's name on the unit.

Last time we saw the experiment. A steady magnetic field through a coil does nothing. A changing one drives a current. To make that exact, we need a way to say how much magnetic field passes through a loop of wire.

Think of an open window in the rain. How much rain comes through? That depends on three things. How hard it is raining. How big the window is. And how the window is facing: tilt it edge-on to the rain, and almost nothing comes through.

A loop of wire in a magnetic field is the same. The amount of field passing through it depends on how strong the field is, how large the loop is, and how the loop is turned. We call that amount the magnetic flux. For a loop facing the field squarely, it is simply the field multiplied by the area.

If you picture the field as lines, the flux is the number of lines passing through the loop.

Its unit is the weber, named after Wilhelm Weber, whom we met in the last video measuring the constants of electricity. One tesla, spread over one square metre, is one weber.

## 3. Faraday's law

**On screen:** Timeline: 1831 again. Three small experiments side by side, each with a loop and a meter. In the first, a magnet approaches and the field through the loop strengthens. In the second, the loop is stretched wider. In the third, the loop is turned. In all three the needle kicks, and only while the change is happening. Then one loop becomes a coil of many turns and the reading multiplies. *EMF = N × rate of change of flux*.

Now we can state what Faraday found, in one line.

Whenever the flux through a loop changes, a voltage appears around the loop. And the faster the flux changes, the larger the voltage.

Notice that it does not matter how you change the flux. You can make the field stronger or weaker, by bringing a magnet closer or switching on a current nearby. You can change the area of the loop. Or you can turn the loop, so that it faces the field more squarely, or less. The loop cannot tell the difference. Any change in flux gives a voltage.

And it is the rate that counts. Push a magnet into a coil slowly, and the needle barely stirs. Push it in fast, and it jumps. The total change is the same. The speed is not.

Engineers call this voltage an electromotive force, or EMF. It is not a force at all, but the old name has stuck. It means the voltage that drives current round a circuit.

There is one more thing you can do. Use a coil instead of a single loop. Every turn has the same flux changing through it, so every turn gets the same voltage, and they add. A hundred turns give a hundred times the voltage.

So Faraday's law says: the EMF equals the number of turns, times the rate of change of flux.

## 4. Lenz's law

**On screen:** Timeline: 1834. A magnet approaching a loop, north pole first. The induced current makes the loop's near face a north pole too, pushing back. The magnet withdrawing: the current reverses, and the loop pulls on it. A hand pushing the magnet, with an arrow for the work done. **Hero scene:** a strong magnet dropped down a thick copper tube, drifting down slowly as rings of current bloom in the copper around it, beside a plain steel ball falling freely.

Which way does the induced current flow? We met the answer in the video on inductance. It is Lenz's law: the induced current always flows so as to oppose the change that caused it.

Push the north pole of a magnet toward a loop. The current that flows turns the near face of the loop into a north pole as well, so the loop pushes back on the magnet. Pull the magnet away, and the current reverses. Now the loop attracts the magnet, trying to hold on to it.

Either way, you feel resistance. You have to do work to move the magnet. And that is exactly where the electrical energy comes from. The work your hand does becomes the energy of the current.

Suppose it went the other way. Suppose the induced current helped the magnet along. Then the magnet would speed up, induce more current, and speed up again, for ever, giving out energy from nothing. Lenz's law is simply the conservation of energy, written for magnets and wires.

You can see it with a strong magnet and a thick copper pipe. Copper is not magnetic. Yet drop the magnet down the pipe, and it does not fall. It drifts down, slowly, as if through honey. The falling magnet changes the flux through each ring of the pipe. Currents flow in the copper, and they oppose the fall.

## 5. A wire moving through a field

**On screen:** Timeline: 1831, October. A straight wire sliding along two rails through a uniform field. Inside the wire, the free electrons are carried sideways with it, and each is pushed along the wire by the magnetic force from the third video. Charge piles up at one end. *EMF = B × L × v*. **Hero scene:** Faraday's disc: a copper disc turning between the poles of a great horseshoe magnet, with one sliding contact at the rim and one at the axle, and a steady current flowing. Then an airliner in flight, with a small voltage marked from wingtip to wingtip.

There is a second way to look at all this, and it ties back to something we already know.

Take a straight piece of wire and move it sideways through a magnetic field. The wire is full of free electrons, and they are being carried along with it. So they are charges moving through a magnetic field. And in the video on magnetism, we saw that such a charge feels a force, at right angles to its motion.

That force pushes the electrons along the wire, toward one end. One end becomes negative, the other positive. A voltage has appeared across the wire.

Its size is easy to remember. It is the field, times the length of the wire, times the speed.

This is induction by motion, and it is how Faraday built the first generator. In October of eighteen thirty-one, he mounted a copper disc so that its edge turned between the poles of a large magnet. One contact rubbed on the rim, another on the axle. When he turned the handle, a steady current flowed. It was feeble. But for the first time, motion was being turned directly into a continuous electric current.

The same thing happens on a grand scale every day. An airliner is a metal bar forty metres long, flying at high speed through the Earth's magnetic field. Between its wingtips there is a voltage: only a fraction of a volt, but real.

## 6. The generator

**On screen:** Timeline: 1832. A rectangular loop turning steadily between two poles. Beside it, the flux through the loop drawn as it turns: a smooth wave. Below that, the voltage: the same wave, shifted, largest when the loop is edge-on and the flux is changing fastest, zero when the loop is face-on. The loop keeps turning and the sine wave rolls out along the screen. Then Pixii's hand-cranked machine. Then a modern turbine hall, the rotor turning fifty times a second.

Now make it practical. Take a loop of wire, and spin it steadily in a magnetic field.

As the loop turns, the flux through it rises and falls. When it faces the field squarely, the flux is greatest. A quarter turn later, it is edge-on, and the flux is zero. Another quarter turn, and the field passes through from the other side.

But remember, the voltage depends on how fast the flux is changing, not on how large it is. When the loop is face-on, the flux is at its peak, and for that instant it is hardly changing at all. So the voltage is zero. When the loop is edge-on, the flux is passing through zero and changing as fast as it ever does. So the voltage is at its greatest.

Keep turning, and the voltage swings smoothly one way, then the other, once in every revolution. That is alternating current. The mains supply is a sine wave for one simple reason: it is made by something going round.

Spin the loop fifty times a second, and you get fifty cycles a second. That is where the frequency of the grid comes from.

The first machine of this kind was built in Paris in eighteen thirty-two, by an instrument maker named Hippolyte Pixii, a few months after Faraday published. It was turned by hand. A modern generator in a power station works in exactly the same way. It is simply larger, by a factor of about a hundred million.

## 7. The transformer

**On screen:** Timeline: 1885, 1886, 1895. Two coils on one iron core. An alternating current in the first, the flux in the core swelling and reversing, and a voltage appearing in the second. The turns of the second coil double and its voltage doubles. *Vs / Vp = Ns / Np*. Then a long transmission line carrying the same power twice: at low voltage with a thick stream of current and the wire glowing with wasted heat; at ten times the voltage with a thin stream and a hundredth of the loss. A map: power station, step up, long line, step down, street, house.

Faraday's ring, with its two coils on one iron core, was not just an experiment. It was the first transformer.

Put an alternating current through the first coil. It makes an alternating flux in the iron. That same flux passes through the second coil, and because it is always changing, it induces a voltage there.

Each turn of the second coil gets the same voltage as each turn of the first, because the same flux passes through both. So the ratio of the voltages is simply the ratio of the turns. Twice as many turns on the second coil gives twice the voltage. Half as many gives half.

You do not get something for nothing. The power going in equals the power coming out, apart from small losses. So if the voltage goes up ten times, the current comes down ten times.

And that is the reason the whole world uses alternating current. The heat wasted in a transmission line depends on the square of the current. Send the same power at ten times the voltage, and the current is a tenth, and the loss is a hundredth.

So we generate at a moderate voltage, step it up to hundreds of thousands of volts for the long journey, and step it down again, in stages, to something safe for a house.

A transformer cannot do this with direct current. A steady current gives a steady flux, and a steady flux induces nothing. That one fact decided the great argument of the eighteen eighties between direct and alternating current. Practical transformers were built in Budapest in eighteen eighty-five, and in the United States by William Stanley the year after. By eighteen ninety-five, the falls at Niagara were driving alternating current generators, and the pattern of every power system since was set.

## 8. Eddy currents

**On screen:** Timeline: 1855. A solid copper plate swinging like a pendulum into the gap of a magnet, and stopping almost dead; whirlpools of current drawn in the plate. The same plate cut into a comb of slots, swinging freely through. Then, quickly: the brake fins of a roller coaster passing between magnets; the base of the induction pan from the last video; a transformer core shown as a stack of thin insulated sheets, with the whirlpools confined to each sheet.

Induction does not need a neat coil of wire. Any piece of metal will do.

Swing a solid copper plate through the gap of a strong magnet. As it enters, the flux through the metal changes, and currents swirl inside the plate itself, like whirlpools in a stream. They are called eddy currents, and they were studied by Léon Foucault in eighteen fifty-five. By Lenz's law, they oppose the motion. The plate stops as if it had hit something soft.

Sometimes that is exactly what we want. Roller coasters and high-speed trains use it for braking: there is nothing to wear out, because nothing touches. An induction cooker uses eddy currents to heat the pan. A metal detector finds a coin by the eddy currents it sets up in it.

And sometimes it is a nuisance. The iron core of a transformer is metal too, sitting in a changing flux. If it were solid, eddy currents would swirl through it and waste energy as heat. So the core is built from thin sheets of steel, each insulated from the next. The currents are confined to narrow slices, and the loss falls to a small fraction.

## 9. Motors, and the series in one picture

**On screen:** The turning loop from the generator, now driven as a motor: current in, torque out, and as it spins up, an opposing voltage rising inside it and the current falling. A stalled motor, its windings glowing. An electric car slowing down, with current flowing back into its battery. Then the coil alone, changing its own flux: the self-induction of the fourth video.

A motor and a generator are the same machine. Push current through the loop, and the field turns it: that is a motor. Turn the loop by hand, and it gives out current: that is a generator.

In fact, it is always both at once. As a motor spins, its coils are turning in a magnetic field, so a voltage is induced in them. By Lenz's law, it opposes the supply. It is called the back EMF, and it grows with speed.

This explains something every engineer has to know. At full speed, the back EMF nearly balances the supply, and the motor draws only a small current. But at the moment of starting, or if the shaft is jammed, there is no back EMF at all. The current is limited only by the resistance of the windings, and it can be many times the normal value. That is why large motors need starters, and why a stalled motor burns out.

It also works in your favour. When an electric car or train slows down, its motor is being driven by the wheels. It becomes a generator, and sends energy back to the battery or the line.

And the inductance we met two videos ago is the same law again. A coil sits in its own flux. Change its current, and you change that flux, and the coil induces a voltage in itself.

Magnetic flux is the amount of field passing through a loop.

Whenever the flux changes, a voltage is induced, equal to the number of turns times the rate of change. That is Faraday's law.

The induced current always opposes the change. That is Lenz's law, and it is the conservation of energy.

Spin a coil in a field, and you have a generator, and alternating current. Link two coils with a changing flux, and you have a transformer.
