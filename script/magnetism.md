# The Second Field: Magnetism

## 1. The needle that moved

**On screen:** Timeline: 1820. A lecture bench in Copenhagen: a battery, a wire, and a compass lying under the wire. The switch closes and the needle swings round to sit across the wire. The switch opens and it swings back.

In the spring of eighteen twenty, a Danish professor named Hans Christian Oersted was giving an evening lecture. On his bench lay a battery, a length of wire, and, by chance, a compass.

He closed the circuit. And the compass needle moved.

Nothing was touching it. No magnet was near it. A current in a wire had reached out and turned a magnet.

For two thousand years, people had treated electricity and magnetism as two separate things. That evening, in front of a few students, the wall between them cracked. To see why it mattered, we have to go back to the beginning.

## 2. The stone that points

**On screen:** Timeline: about 600 BC, 1088, 1269, 1600. A dark lodestone lifting iron nails. A Chinese spoon of lodestone turning on a bronze plate, then a needle floating in a bowl and a ship taking its bearing. Peregrinus's round magnet with a needle laid on it at many points, the lines traced on its surface meeting at two spots. **Hero scene:** Gilbert's terrella, a small magnetised sphere, with tiny needles all over it dipping and pointing, dissolving into the Earth itself with a compass at the same angles.

The ancient Greeks knew of a dark stone, found near a place called Magnesia, that pulled on iron. We call it lodestone, and from Magnesia we get the word magnet.

In China, a sliver of lodestone was found to turn until it lay north and south. By the year ten eighty-eight, the scholar Shen Kuo had described a magnetised needle used to find direction, and noted that it did not point exactly true. Within a century, sailors were crossing open sea by it.

The first careful experiments came in twelve sixty-nine, from a French soldier and engineer, Peter Peregrinus. He laid a needle on a round lodestone at many places, traced the lines it followed, and found that they all met at two points. He named them poles. He showed that like poles push apart and unlike poles pull together. And he found something strange: break a magnet in half, and you do not get one pole on each piece. You get two complete magnets.

Then, in sixteen hundred, William Gilbert, the same man who gave us the word electric, carved a lodestone into a small sphere and studied how a needle behaved on its surface. It behaved exactly as a compass does on the Earth. His conclusion was bold. The Earth itself is a great magnet.

## 3. Poles, and why they come in pairs

**On screen:** A bar magnet with a north and a south end. It is cut in two, and each half shows both poles; cut again, and again, down to the smallest sliver. Beside it, a positive and a negative charge drift apart on their own.

Every magnet has two poles, north and south. And here magnetism differs sharply from electricity.

A positive charge can exist alone. So can a negative one. But nobody has ever found a north pole on its own. Cut a magnet as small as you like, and each piece still has both.

That is a clue. It tells us that magnetism does not come from some kind of magnetic charge. It comes from something else. Hold on to that question.

---

## 4. The magnetic field

**On screen:** Iron filings shaken over a bar magnet settle into arcs. The arcs become glowing lines leaving the north end, curving round, entering the south end, and running back through the magnet to close on themselves. A small compass carried around the magnet turns to follow them. Then the Earth, with the same pattern on a vast scale.

Like a charge, a magnet changes the space around it. Scatter iron filings near one and they line up along curves, showing a pattern that was there all along. That pattern is the magnetic field, and we write it with the letter B.

We draw it with lines, as Faraday taught us. By convention, they leave the north pole and enter the south. A compass needle placed anywhere in the field turns to lie along the line through that point.

But there is one great difference from the electric field. Electric field lines start on a positive charge and end on a negative one. Magnetic field lines never start and never end. They pass through the magnet and close on themselves, in unbroken loops. That is the same fact as before, seen another way: there are no single poles for the lines to begin or end on.

## 5. Current makes magnetism

**On screen:** Timeline: 1820 again, twice more. Oersted's wire, now with rings of field around it shown by a circle of small compasses. A right hand gripping the wire, thumb along the current, fingers curling the way the field goes. Then Ampère's two parallel wires: with currents the same way they bow toward each other, with currents opposed they spring apart. *B = μ₀ I / 2π r*.

Now we can see what Oersted found. A wire carrying a current is surrounded by a magnetic field. The field does not point toward the wire, or away from it. It circles the wire, in rings.

There is an easy way to remember the direction. Grip the wire in your right hand, with your thumb pointing along the current. Your fingers curl the way the field goes.

The news reached Paris in September. André-Marie Ampère saw it demonstrated, and within one week he had gone further. If a current acts like a magnet, he reasoned, then two currents should act on each other. He set up two parallel wires. With the currents flowing the same way, the wires pulled together. With the currents opposed, they pushed apart. No iron, no lodestone. Only electricity in motion.

That same autumn, Biot and Savart measured the strength of the field. Around a long straight wire, it grows with the current and falls off with distance. One ampere, at a distance of one centimetre, gives about twenty millionths of a tesla: roughly half the strength of the Earth's own field.

Ampère drew the conclusion that changed everything. Magnetism is not a separate force. It is what moving charge does.

## 6. Coils and electromagnets

**On screen:** Timeline: 1825, 1831. A straight wire bent into a single loop, its field rings merging into one pattern through the middle. Then many loops side by side: a solenoid, with a field through its core exactly like a bar magnet's. An iron bar slides in and the field leaps up. **Hero scene:** Joseph Henry's great horseshoe electromagnet hoisting a platform loaded with more than a ton of iron weights. *B = μ₀ n I*.

Bend the wire into a loop, and the rings of field add up through the middle. One side of the loop behaves like a north pole, the other like a south. A loop of current is a magnet.

Wind many loops side by side into a coil, called a solenoid, and their fields add together. Inside, the field runs straight and nearly uniform. Outside, it is exactly the field of a bar magnet. The more turns in each metre, and the more current, the stronger it is.

Then put a bar of iron inside. In eighteen twenty-five, William Sturgeon did just that, and his small coil lifted twenty times its own weight. Six years later, the American Joseph Henry, winding many layers of insulated wire, built an electromagnet that held up more than a ton.

A magnet you can switch on and off. Every relay, every contactor, every motor and every transformer begins here.

## 7. Why iron?

**On screen:** Timeline: 1895, 1907. Inside iron: each atom a tiny loop of current with a small arrow. The arrows gather into patches, each patch pointing its own way: domains. A field is applied and the patches swing into line and grow. Then the iron is heated to a dull red, the arrows scatter, and the magnetism is gone. *770 °C*.

So why does iron do this, when copper and wood do not? And where is the current in a lump of lodestone?

Ampère guessed the answer, long before anyone could check it. He proposed that inside a magnet there are countless tiny loops of current. He was right. Electrons circle their atoms, and they also spin, and each one acts as a minute magnet.

In most materials these cancel out. But in iron, nickel and cobalt, neighbouring atoms line up with each other. In nineteen hundred and seven, Pierre Weiss showed that they do so in patches, called domains. In an ordinary piece of iron, the domains point in all directions, and the whole is not a magnet. Apply a field, and the domains swing into line. The iron's own magnetism adds to the field, hundreds or thousands of times over.

That is why an iron core makes an electromagnet so strong. And it explains our earlier puzzle. Cut a magnet in half, and you are only cutting between loops of current. Each piece still has both faces.

Heat destroys the order. In eighteen ninety-five, Pierre Curie found that above a certain temperature, seven hundred and seventy degrees for iron, the alignment collapses, and a magnet stops being a magnet.

## 8. The force on a moving charge

**On screen:** An electron fired into a uniform field, its path bending into a circle. The force arrow always at right angles to its motion. Then a wire in the field between two magnet poles, carrying a current, kicked sideways. A left hand with thumb, first finger and second finger at right angles. A loop of wire in the field, turning: the beginning of a motor. *F = q v B* and *F = B I L*.

A magnetic field does not push on a charge that is standing still. It acts only on a charge that is moving. And the force is strange: it points neither along the field nor along the motion, but at right angles to both.

A force that is always sideways cannot speed a charge up or slow it down. It can only turn it. So a charge moving across a magnetic field travels in a circle.

A current is nothing but charges in motion. So a wire carrying current across a field is pushed sideways. The force is the field, times the current, times the length of wire in the field.

Put a loop of wire in a field and pass a current through it. One side is pushed up, the other down, and the loop turns. You are looking at an electric motor.

## 9. Measuring it

**On screen:** Timeline: 1832, 1948, 1960, 2019. Gauss's observatory needle swinging on a thread. A scale of field strengths rising from the Earth's field through a fridge magnet and an electromagnet to an MRI scanner. Then two long parallel wires one metre apart, with the tiny force between them marked.

To build with magnetism, we have to measure it. In eighteen thirty-two, the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss worked out how to measure the Earth's field in absolute terms, using only length, mass and time. The old unit of field carries his name.

The modern unit is the tesla, after Nikola Tesla, adopted by international agreement in nineteen sixty. One tesla is ten thousand gauss, and it is a strong field. The Earth's field is about fifty millionths of a tesla. A fridge magnet, a few thousandths. A hospital scanner, between one and a half and three.

And magnetism gave us the unit of current itself. From nineteen forty-eight, the ampere was defined by Ampère's own experiment: the current which, in two long parallel wires a metre apart, produces a set force between them. It stayed that way until twenty nineteen, when the ampere was redefined by counting electrons.

## 10. Recap

**On screen:** The timeline, complete. Each idea returns as one image: the lodestone, the cut magnet, the closed loops, the rings round the wire, the coil, the domains, the circling electron. End card: Like · Share · Subscribe, with the channel handle @electricalmcqs.

So let's put it together.

Magnets have two poles, and the poles always come in pairs.

A magnet fills the space around it with a magnetic field, whose lines form closed loops.

A current makes a magnetic field that circles the wire. Magnetism is what moving charge does.

A coil concentrates that field, and an iron core multiplies it, because iron is full of tiny current loops that line up.

A magnetic field pushes sideways on moving charge, and that push turns every motor.

So a current makes a magnetic field. Next time, we ask what happens when that field has to be built up, and torn down again. The answer is called inductance.
