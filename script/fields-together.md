# Two Fields, One Light: How Electric and Magnetic Fields Are Related

## 1. Two fields

**On screen:** The screen split in two. On the left, a charge with its electric field lines reaching outward. On the right, a wire with its rings of magnetic field. Then the two rules written side by side: *charge makes an electric field*; *moving charge makes a magnetic field*. A question mark appears between the two fields themselves. Last, a phone lying on a charging pad, a radio mast, and the Sun.

Over the last four videos, we have met two fields.

The electric field is made by charge. Its lines begin on positive charge and end on negative. It pushes on any charge, whether that charge is moving or not. It is the field between the plates of a capacitor.

The magnetic field is made by charge in motion. Its lines have no beginning and no end. It pushes only on charge that is moving. It is the field in and around a coil.

So far, the only thing connecting them is charge. A charge makes one field. Set it moving, and it makes the other as well.

But here is a deeper question. Can the fields act on each other directly, with no charge in between? Can one field make the other?

The answer took thirty years to find. It explains how a phone charges on a pad without any contacts, how a radio signal crosses an ocean, and what light actually is.

## 2. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field

**On screen:** Timeline: 1831. Faraday's iron ring with two separate coils, a battery on one and a needle on the other. The switch closes: the needle kicks, then returns to rest. The switch opens: it kicks the other way. Then a bar magnet pushed into a coil: a kick; held still: nothing; pulled out: a kick the other way. Then the wire fades away and the picture stays: a magnetic field growing through empty space, and around it, closed loops of electric field, circling. Last, an induction cooker: a coil under the glass, a pan above it, loops of current glowing in the base of the pan.

The first half of the answer came from Michael Faraday. Once Oersted had shown that electricity makes magnetism, Faraday was sure the reverse must be possible. He wrote himself a note: convert magnetism into electricity. It took him ten years.

In August of eighteen thirty-one, he wound two separate coils on an iron ring. One went to a battery. The other went to a meter. There was no connection between them.

With a steady current in the first coil, the meter showed nothing at all. That was why everyone had failed. They had been looking for a steady effect. But Faraday noticed that at the instant he switched the current on, the needle kicked. Then it settled back. And when he switched off, it kicked the other way.

You can see the same thing with a bar magnet and a coil of wire. Push the magnet in, and a current flows. Hold it still, however strong it is, and nothing happens. Pull it out, and the current flows the other way.

A steady magnetic field does nothing. Only a changing one drives a current.

Now think about what that means. The charges in that wire were sitting at rest, and something pushed them. A magnetic field cannot push a charge at rest. Only an electric field can. So a changing magnetic field must create an electric field.

And it is an electric field of a new kind. The fields we met before start on one charge and end on another. This one has no charges to start or end on. Its lines close on themselves, in loops, circling the changing magnetic field.

The wire only reveals it. Take the wire away, and the loops of electric field are still there, in empty space.

You may have this in your kitchen. Under the glass of an induction cooker is a coil carrying a rapidly changing current. Its changing magnetic field passes up through the glass, and creates loops of electric field in the base of the pan. Those drive currents in the metal, and the pan heats itself. The glass stays cool, because there is nothing in glass for the field to push around.

## 3. The missing piece

**On screen:** Timeline: 1861. Maxwell at a desk, with four statements on a sheet in front of him; three are ticked, one has a gap. Then a capacitor being charged through a wire. Rings of magnetic field around the wire, as always. The rings stop dead at the plate: in the gap there is no current. A question mark over the gap. Then the electric field between the plates, growing; and around that growing field, rings of magnetic field appear, joining up smoothly with the ones around the wire.

Faraday was a brilliant experimenter, but he had almost no mathematics. Thirty years later, a Scottish physicist named James Clerk Maxwell set out to turn Faraday's pictures into equations.

He wrote down everything that was known, and he found that the rules did not quite fit together. The trouble showed up in a capacitor.

Think of a capacitor being charged. Current flows along the wire, and around the wire there is a magnetic field, in rings, exactly as Oersted found. Now follow the wire to the plate. Between the plates there is a gap. No charge crosses it. There is no current there at all.

So what happens to the magnetic field? Does it simply stop, at the edge of the plate, and start again on the other side? A field cannot be cut off like that.

But look at what is in the gap. It is not empty. As the plates charge, the electric field between them is growing stronger every instant.

Maxwell proposed that this is enough. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field around it, just as a current does. With that one addition, the rings of magnetic field carry on smoothly around the gap, and the rules fit together everywhere.

Nobody had ever seen this effect. It is far too small to detect with a compass and a capacitor. Maxwell added it because the mathematics demanded it, and because of what it led to next.

## 4. Each makes the other

**On screen:** The two statements side by side, mirror images: *a changing magnetic field makes an electric field*; *a changing electric field makes a magnetic field*. A row of dominoes falling, each tipping the next. Then a single charge is shaken once. **Hero scene:** a ripple leaves it: a loop of electric field, which raises a loop of magnetic field beside it, which raises electric field beyond that, the two at right angles, marching outward through empty space long after the charge has stopped. Then a rope being flicked, with a single hump running along it.

Now put the two halves side by side.

A changing magnetic field makes an electric field. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field.

Do you see what follows? Take a charge and give it a shake.

Its electric field changes. That change makes a magnetic field around it. But a moment ago that magnetic field was not there, so it is changing too. And a changing magnetic field makes an electric field, a little further out. Which is new, and changing, and so makes a magnetic field beyond that.

It is like a row of dominoes, where each one falling tips the next. Except that here there are no dominoes. Each field creates the other, one step further on, in empty space.

So the disturbance does not stay near the charge. It travels outward by itself. The charge can stop moving, and the ripple carries on.

Maxwell had found, on paper, an electromagnetic wave.

It has a particular shape. Flick a rope, and the rope moves up and down while the hump travels along. In the same way, the electric field in the wave points across the direction of travel. The magnetic field points across it too, at right angles to the electric field. And the two rise and fall together, in step.

Unlike the hump on a rope, though, this wave needs nothing to travel in. The fields carry themselves.

## 5. The speed

**On screen:** Timeline: 1849, 1856, 1862. Two constants from the earlier videos return: the one from the capacitor formula and the one from the coil formula, each beside a laboratory bench with plates and a coil. They combine into a single number, a speed, which rolls up to 310,740 kilometres per second. Then Fizeau's apparatus: a beam of light passing through the teeth of a spinning wheel, out to a mirror on a distant hill and back. His figure: 314,858. The two numbers sit one above the other.

How fast does such a wave travel? Maxwell could work it out, and he needed only two numbers. We have already met both.

The first appeared in the formula for a capacitor. It tells us how strong the electric field of a given charge is. The second appeared in the formula for a coil. It tells us how strong the magnetic field of a given current is.

Neither has anything to do with waves, or with speed. You measure them on a bench, with metal plates, coils of wire, and a sensitive balance. Two German physicists, Weber and Kohlrausch, had done exactly that in eighteen fifty-six.

Maxwell put their figures into his equations. Out came a speed: three hundred and ten thousand kilometres every second.

He had seen a number like that before. A few years earlier, in France, Hippolyte Fizeau had sent a beam of light through the teeth of a spinning wheel to a mirror eight kilometres away, and timed its journey there and back. His result for the speed of light was three hundred and fifteen thousand kilometres a second.

One number came from charged plates and coils. The other came from a light beam and a toothed wheel. They agreed to within two percent.

Maxwell wrote that we can scarcely avoid the conclusion that light itself is an electromagnetic wave.

Until then, optics, electricity and magnetism had been three separate sciences. From that moment, they were one.

## 6. Hertz

**On screen:** Timeline: 1887, 1901. A darkened laboratory in Karlsruhe. **Hero scene:** on one bench, two brass rods with a small gap, sparking; across the room, a plain loop of wire with a tiny gap of its own, and in that gap, a faint answering spark. A wave drawn between them. A metal sheet reflecting the wave. Then waves of different lengths drawn to scale against familiar objects: a building, a hand, a pinhead, a cell, an atom. The spectrum unrolling: radio, microwaves, infrared, the thin band of visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays.

But it was still only a prediction. Maxwell died in eighteen seventy-nine, without seeing it tested. The proof came eight years later, from a young German professor, Heinrich Hertz.

If Maxwell was right, then charge rushing back and forth should send out waves. So on one side of his laboratory, Hertz set up two brass rods with a small gap between them, and made a spark leap back and forth across it, many millions of times a second.

On the other side of the room, he held a simple loop of wire with a tiny gap of its own. It was connected to nothing. No battery, no wires.

When the first spark fired, a faint spark appeared in the gap of the loop.

Something had crossed the room. Hertz went on to test it as you would test light. A metal sheet reflected it, like a mirror. A curved one focused it. And when he measured its speed, it was the speed of light.

Asked what use his discovery might be, he is said to have replied: none at all. He was wrong about that. In nineteen hundred and one, Guglielmo Marconi sent a signal across the Atlantic. Today the unit of frequency, one cycle each second, is called the hertz.

Hertz's waves were a few metres long. Light waves are less than a thousandth of a millimetre long. That is the only difference between them.

Radio waves, as long as a building. Microwaves, the size of your hand, which is why they fit inside an oven. Infrared, which you feel as warmth. The narrow band that your eyes can detect. Ultraviolet. X-rays, smaller than an atom. They are all the same thing: electric and magnetic fields, each creating the other, crossing space at the same speed.

## 7. One field, two faces

**On screen:** Timeline: 1884, 1905. A charge sitting on a table, with only an electric field around it; an observer stands beside it holding a compass that does not move. A second observer walks past: from their side, the charge is moving, and rings of magnetic field appear around its path; their compass swings. The view cuts back and forth between the two. Then Maxwell's long page of twenty equations folding down into four short lines, signed by Heaviside.

There is one more step, and it is the strangest.

Picture a charge sitting on a table. You are standing beside it. Around it there is an electric field, and no magnetic field at all. A compass in your hand does nothing.

Now a friend walks past the table. From where they are, the charge is moving. And we learned that a moving charge makes a magnetic field. So your friend's compass should swing.

Who is right? Is there a magnetic field or not?

Both are right. In nineteen hundred and five, Albert Einstein opened his most famous paper with exactly this puzzle. His answer was that the electric field and the magnetic field are not two separate things. They are two faces of one thing, the electromagnetic field. How much of it appears as electric, and how much as magnetic, depends on how you are moving.

It is a little like looking at a coin. From the front you see a circle. From the edge you see a line. The coin has not changed. You have.

That is why the two fields are bound so tightly together. They were never really two.

The equations that describe this single field were tidied into the form we use today by Oliver Heaviside, in eighteen eighty-four. He reduced Maxwell's twenty equations to four short lines. Every electrical machine ever built obeys them.

## 8. What engineers do with it

**On screen:** A phone on a wireless charging pad, shown in section: a coil in the pad, a coil in the phone, and the changing field linking them. A transformer on a pole, the same idea at a larger scale. An antenna with current surging up and down it and waves peeling away, and a second antenna far off. A signal pulse travelling along a cable, with the field between the conductors moving with it. A power line, with a wavelength marker stretching away over the horizon: six thousand kilometres.

For an engineer, this changes how you look at every circuit.

Start with something small. A wireless charging pad has a coil in it, carrying a changing current. Your phone has a second coil in its back. The changing magnetic field of the first creates an electric field in the second, and drives a current that charges the battery. Nothing touches. The energy crosses the gap as field.

A transformer is the same idea, built to carry the power of a whole town. Two coils that never touch, linked only by a changing magnetic field.

An antenna goes one step further. Charge is driven up and down a wire so quickly that its fields cannot keep up. They break away and travel off as waves. Far away, a second antenna sits in the passing wave, and the wave's electric field pushes its charges back and forth. That tiny current is your radio signal.

Even in an ordinary cable, the picture changes. The energy does not really travel inside the copper. It travels in the fields around and between the two conductors, guided by them, at close to the speed of light. The electrons themselves, as we saw in the first video, barely crawl.

And that speed, though enormous, is not infinite. At fifty cycles a second, one wavelength is six thousand kilometres long. On a very long transmission line, the voltage at the far end is noticeably out of step with the voltage at the near end. Power engineers have to design for that.

So let's put it together.

Charge makes an electric field. Moving charge makes a magnetic field.

A changing magnetic field makes an electric field. That was Faraday.

A changing electric field makes a magnetic field. That was Maxwell.

Because each makes the other, the two can travel through empty space together as a wave. Hertz found that wave. Light is one kind of it.

And the two fields are really two faces of one electromagnetic field.

One of those links matters more to an electrical engineer than any other: a changing magnetic field makes a voltage. Next time, in the last video of this series, we take it apart properly. It is called electromagnetic induction, and it runs every generator and every transformer on Earth.
