# Two Fields, One Light: How Electric and Magnetic Fields Are Related

Lines in plain text are spoken. Lines marked **On screen** describe the picture and are not spoken. **Hero scene** marks a showpiece shot. A timeline strip runs along the bottom of the frame, as in the earlier videos.

---

## 1. Two fields

**On screen:** The screen split in two. On the left, a charge with its electric field lines reaching outward. On the right, a wire with its rings of magnetic field. Between them, a question mark. Then the two rules written side by side: *charge makes an electric field*; *moving charge makes a magnetic field*.

We now have two fields.

An electric field, made by charge. Its lines begin on positive charge and end on negative. It pushes on any charge, moving or not.

And a magnetic field, made by charge in motion. Its lines have no beginning and no end. It pushes only on charge that is moving.

So far, they are connected through charge. A charge makes one. Set it moving, and it makes the other as well.

But here is a deeper question. Can the fields act on each other directly, with no charge in between? Can one field make the other?

The answer took thirty years to find. It turned out to explain light itself.

---

## 2. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field

**On screen:** Timeline: 1831. Faraday's iron ring with two separate coils, a battery on one and a needle on the other. The switch closes: the needle kicks, then returns. The switch opens: it kicks the other way. Then the wire fades away and the picture stays: a magnetic field growing through empty space, and around it, closed loops of electric field, circling.

The first half came from Michael Faraday. Once Oersted had shown that electricity makes magnetism, Faraday was sure the reverse must be possible. He searched for ten years.

In August of eighteen thirty-one, he wound two separate coils on an iron ring. One went to a battery. The other went to a meter. With a steady current in the first coil, the meter showed nothing. But at the instant he switched the current on, the needle kicked. And when he switched it off, it kicked the other way.

A steady magnetic field did nothing. A changing one drove a current.

Look at what that means. Charges in the second coil were pushed around the wire. Only an electric field pushes on a charge at rest. So a changing magnetic field must create an electric field.

And it is an electric field of a new kind. Its lines do not start or end on any charge. They close on themselves, in loops, around the changing magnetic field. The wire is not needed. The loops are there even in empty space.

## 3. The missing piece

**On screen:** Timeline: 1861. A capacitor being charged through a wire. Rings of magnetic field around the wire, as always. The rings stop at the plate: in the gap there is no current. A question mark over the gap. Then the electric field between the plates, growing; and around that growing field, the rings of magnetic field appear, joining up with the ones around the wire.

Thirty years later, a Scottish physicist named James Clerk Maxwell was writing all of this down in mathematics. And he found that the rules did not quite fit together.

Think of a capacitor being charged. Current flows along the wire, and around the wire there is a magnetic field. But between the plates there is a gap. No charge crosses it. So does the magnetic field simply stop at the edge of the plate?

That made no sense. But notice what is in the gap. As the plates charge, the electric field between them is growing.

Maxwell proposed that a changing electric field makes a magnetic field, just as a current does. With that one addition, the rings of magnetic field continue smoothly around the gap, and the rules fit together everywhere.

Nobody had seen this effect. It was far too small to measure with the instruments of eighteen sixty-one. Maxwell added it because the mathematics demanded it.

## 4. Each makes the other

**On screen:** The two statements side by side, mirror images: *a changing magnetic field makes an electric field*; *a changing electric field makes a magnetic field*. Then a single charge is shaken once. **Hero scene:** a ripple leaves it: a loop of electric field, which raises a loop of magnetic field beside it, which raises electric field beyond that, the two at right angles, marching outward through empty space long after the charge has stopped.

Now put the two halves side by side.

A changing magnetic field makes an electric field. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field.

Do you see what follows? Shake a charge. Its electric field changes. That change makes a magnetic field. But this magnetic field is new, so it is changing too, and it makes an electric field a little further out. Which makes a magnetic field beyond that.

Each field keeps creating the other, one step further on. The disturbance does not stay near the charge. It travels outward, on its own, through empty space. It needs no wire and no material to carry it.

Maxwell had found, on paper, an electromagnetic wave. The electric and magnetic fields in it are at right angles to each other, and both are at right angles to the direction of travel.

## 5. The speed

**On screen:** Timeline: 1856, 1862. Two constants from the earlier videos return: the one from the capacitor formula and the one from the coil formula, each measured on a laboratory bench. They combine into a single number, a speed, which rolls up to 310,740 kilometres per second. Beside it, the measured speed of light: 314,858. The two numbers sit one above the other.

How fast does such a wave travel? Maxwell could work it out from two numbers we have already met.

One is the constant in the formula for a capacitor, which sets the strength of electric forces. The other is the constant in the formula for a coil, which sets the strength of magnetic forces. Both can be measured on a bench, with plates and coils and a balance. Two German physicists, Weber and Kohlrausch, had done exactly that in eighteen fifty-six.

Maxwell put their figures into his equations. The speed came out at three hundred and ten thousand kilometres every second.

He knew that number. The speed of light had been measured a few years before, by timing a beam over a known distance. It was three hundred and fifteen thousand.

Two results, from experiments that had nothing to do with each other, agreed to within two percent. Maxwell wrote that we can scarcely avoid the conclusion that light is an electromagnetic wave.

Light, electricity and magnetism: one subject.

## 6. Hertz

**On screen:** Timeline: 1887. A darkened laboratory in Karlsruhe. **Hero scene:** on one bench, two brass rods with a small gap, sparking; across the room, a plain loop of wire with a tiny gap of its own, and in that gap, a faint answering spark. A wave drawn between them. Then the spectrum unrolling: radio, microwaves, infrared, the thin band of visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays.

Maxwell died in eighteen seventy-nine, with his prediction untested. The proof came eight years later, from a young German professor, Heinrich Hertz.

On one side of his laboratory, Hertz made a spark jump back and forth across a gap between two brass rods. On the other side of the room, he held a loop of wire with a tiny gap of its own. Nothing connected them. And yet, when the first spark fired, a faint spark appeared in the gap of the loop.

Something had crossed the room. Hertz showed that it could be reflected, like light. It could be focused, like light. And when he measured its speed, it was the speed of light.

Asked what use his discovery might be, he is said to have replied: none at all. Within fifteen years, signals were crossing the Atlantic. Today the unit of frequency carries his name.

And the waves he made differ from light in only one way: their wavelength. Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays. They are all the same thing.

## 7. One field, two faces

**On screen:** Timeline: 1884, 1905. A charge sitting still, with only an electric field around it. Then the viewpoint glides past it: from here the charge is moving, and rings of magnetic field appear around its path. The viewpoint stops and they vanish. Then Maxwell's long page of equations folding down into four short lines, signed by Heaviside.

There is one more step, and it is the strangest.

Picture a charge sitting still. Around it there is an electric field, and no magnetic field at all.

Now walk past it. From where you are, the charge is moving. And a moving charge has a magnetic field. So for you there is one, and for someone standing beside the charge there is not.

Who is right? Both. In nineteen hundred and five, Albert Einstein began his most famous paper with exactly this puzzle, and his answer was that the electric and magnetic fields are not two separate things. They are two faces of a single thing, the electromagnetic field. How much of each you find depends on how you are moving.

The equations that describe it were tidied into the form we use today by Oliver Heaviside, in eighteen eighty-four. Four short lines. Every electrical machine ever built obeys them.

## 8. What engineers do with it

**On screen:** An antenna with current surging up and down it and waves peeling away. Two coils on one core, with the changing field of the first threading the second. A signal pulse travelling along a cable, with the field between the conductors moving with it. A power line, with a wavelength marker stretching away over the horizon: six thousand kilometres.

For an engineer, this changes how you see every circuit.

An antenna is a wire in which charge is driven back and forth, so that its fields change, and break away as waves. A second antenna, far off, turns the passing wave back into current.

A transformer has two coils that never touch. The changing magnetic field of one creates an electric field that drives current in the other. Energy crosses the gap as field.

Even in a simple cable, the energy does not really travel inside the copper. It travels in the fields around and between the conductors, guided by them, at close to the speed of light.

And that speed is not infinite. At fifty cycles a second, one wavelength is six thousand kilometres. On a very long transmission line, the voltage at the far end can be out of step with the voltage at the near end, and engineers have to design for it.

So let's put it together.

Charge makes an electric field. Moving charge makes a magnetic field.

A changing magnetic field makes an electric field. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field.

Together, the two can travel through empty space as a wave, and that wave is light.

The electric and magnetic fields are two faces of one electromagnetic field.

One of those links matters more to an electrical engineer than any other: a changing magnetic field makes a voltage. Next time, in the last video of this series, we take it apart properly. It is called electromagnetic induction, and it runs every generator and every transformer on Earth.
